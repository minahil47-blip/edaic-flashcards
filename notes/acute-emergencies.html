PE, ACS, stroke, status epilepticus and environmental emergencies

The four acute presentations the anaesthetist meets outside theatre, with the numbers, the time windows and the decisions that change outcome.

Pulmonary embolism

StepDetail
Pretest probabilityWells score. PE unlikely → D-dimer; negative D-dimer excludes it. PE likely → straight to CTPA, because a D-dimer cannot exclude a high-probability PE
D-dimerSensitive but not specific — raised by surgery, pregnancy, malignancy, infection and age. Age-adjusted thresholds improve specificity in the elderly

ImagingCTPA is the standard. V/Q or V/Q SPECT in pregnancy or renal failure; echo for right ventricular strain in the unstable patient who cannot be moved
Risk stratifyHigh-risk = haemodynamic instability → thrombolysis. Intermediate = RV dysfunction or raised troponin → monitor closely. Low risk (PESI/sPESI) → consider outpatient treatment

Treating massive PE

  • Thrombolysis is for haemodynamic instability, not for a large clot burden alone — alteplase, with surgical embolectomy or catheter-directed therapy if thrombolysis is contraindicated or fails.
  • The failing right ventricle is the problem. Avoid fluid overload — it distends the RV and worsens output. Noradrenaline to maintain coronary perfusion of the RV, avoid hypoxia and hypercapnia (both raise PVR), and consider inhaled pulmonary vasodilators.
  • Induction of anaesthesia in massive PE is extremely dangerous — loss of sympathetic tone and the switch to positive pressure ventilation can precipitate arrest.
  • In pregnancy, LMWH is the anticoagulant; warfarin is teratogenic and DOACs contraindicated.

Perioperative myocardial injury is a different animal

  • Type 1 infarction is plaque rupture. Type 2 is supply–demand mismatch — tachycardia, hypotension, anaemia, hypoxia — and is the dominant perioperative mechanism.
  • Most perioperative myocardial injury is silent, masked by analgesia, and detected only by troponin surveillance. It carries a markedly increased 30-day mortality.
  • So the prevention is physiological: avoid tachycardia, hypotension, anaemia and hypoxia, and treat pain. Thrombolysis is contraindicated after surgery; management is medical and interventional.
  • Continue beta blockers, statins and aspirin where possible. Stopping beta blockers abruptly causes rebound; stopping statins increases events.
  • Troponin is cardiac-specific but not infarct-specific — it rises in sepsis, PE, renal failure, arrhythmia and heart failure. The diagnosis needs the rise plus clinical context.

Stroke

  1. 1

    Image first

    Immediate non-contrast CT to exclude haemorrhage before any thrombolysis. Time is the organ — "time is brain".

  2. 2

    Thrombolysis

    Alteplase within 4.5 h of onset for ischaemic stroke, with blood pressure brought below 185/110 first. Recent surgery, active bleeding, anticoagulation and haemorrhagic stroke are contraindications.

  3. 3

    Thrombectomy

    For large-vessel anterior circulation occlusion, within 6 hours, and up to 24 hours in selected patients with favourable perfusion imaging. This is where the anaesthetist is involved.

  4. 4

    Anaesthesia for thrombectomy

    GA or conscious sedation both acceptable; what matters is avoiding hypotension, which is associated with worse outcome. Keep blood pressure at or near baseline, maintain normocapnia and normoglycaemia.

The rest of stroke care

  • Aspirin 300 mg for 2 weeks after ischaemic stroke, then long-term secondary prevention. Not before haemorrhage has been excluded.
  • Permissive hypertension in ischaemic stroke — do not lower blood pressure aggressively, since it maintains perfusion of the penumbra. In haemorrhagic stroke, by contrast, systolic is lowered towards 140 mmHg.
  • Avoid hyperglycaemia and pyrexia; both worsen outcome. Screen swallowing before oral intake.
  • Intermittent pneumatic compression, not graduated stockings, for thromboprophylaxis — CLOTS showed stockings do not work and cause harm after stroke.
  • Subarachnoid haemorrhage: thunderclap headache, CT then LP for xanthochromia; nimodipine for delayed cerebral ischaemia; early coiling or clipping; watch for hyponatraemia (SIADH vs cerebral salt wasting), neurogenic pulmonary oedema and ECG changes.
  • Elective surgery is generally deferred for a period after stroke — the risk of recurrence is highest in the first weeks and falls over months.

Status epilepticus

Definition (ILAE operational): 5 minutes or more of continuous seizure activity, or two or more seizures without recovery between them. The old 30-minute definition is obsolete — treatment starts at 5 minutes.

  1. 1

    0 to 5 min — benzodiazepine

    Airway, oxygen, glucose, thiamine if alcohol is suspected. IV lorazepam 4 mg, or buccal midazolam / rectal diazepam without access. Repeat once after 5 to 10 minutes.

  2. 2

    Established — second-line

    Levetiracetam, sodium valproate or phenytoin — ESETT found all three equally effective, so levetiracetam is often preferred for ease and safety. Phenytoin needs cardiac monitoring and a slow rate (max 50 mg/min) because of hypotension and arrhythmia, and extravasation causes severe tissue injury.

  3. 3

    Refractory — 30+ min

    Intubate, ventilate and give general anaesthesia — propofol, midazolam or thiopentone infusion, titrated with EEG, aiming for seizure suppression or burst suppression. Ketamine is used in super-refractory cases.

  4. 4

    Throughout — look for the cause

    Hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hypocalcaemia, eclampsia, drug toxicity or withdrawal, infection, intracranial lesion, and local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. Eclampsia is treated with magnesium, not a conventional anticonvulsant.

Two traps

  • Paralysis abolishes the movements, not the seizure. A patient given a neuromuscular blocker may continue in non-convulsive status — EEG is then the only way to know, and this is why continuous monitoring is used.
  • Complications of prolonged seizure: lactic acidosis (which corrects quickly and does not need bicarbonate), hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, aspiration, neurogenic pulmonary oedema and raised intracranial pressure.

Drowning, hypothermia & environmental

ConditionWhat matters
DrowningHypoxia is the mechanism, so resuscitation starts with 5 rescue breaths then compressions — an airway-first modification of standard ALS. The old "wet vs dry" and fresh vs salt water distinctions are not clinically useful. Do not attempt to drain water from the lungs
HypothermiaMild 32–35 °C, moderate 28–32 °C, severe < 28 °C. "Not dead until warm and dead" — prolonged resuscitation is justified, and survival after very prolonged arrest with hypothermic drowning is documented. ECMO rewarming is the treatment of choice in severe cases
Hypothermic arrestThe heart is unresponsive to drugs and defibrillation below about 30 °C: limit shocks to three and withhold drugs until rewarmed above 30 °C, then give them at longer intervals. Handle gently — rough movement provokes VF
RewarmingPassive, active external, and active internal (warm fluids, lavage, ECMO). Beware rewarming collapse from vasodilatation of a volume-depleted patient, and the afterdrop as cold peripheral blood returns
Heat strokeCore > 40 °C with CNS dysfunction. Rapid active cooling is the treatment — cold water immersion is most effective. Antipyretics do not work. Dantrolene is not indicated: this is not malignant hyperthermia
Malignant hyperthermiaBy contrast: triggered by volatiles and suxamethonium, rising EtCO₂ is the earliest sign, masseter spasm, rigidity, hyperkalaemia, acidosis, rhabdomyolysis. Dantrolene 2.5 mg/kg repeated to 10 mg/kg, stop trigger, 100% O₂, cool, treat potassium
ElectrocutionAC causes tetany and VF; DC throws. Look for deep tissue injury, rhabdomyolysis and spinal injury from the fall
AnaphylaxisIM adrenaline 0.5 mg (0.5 mL of 1:1000) in the anterolateral thigh, repeated at 5 min; IV only by those experienced, in 50 µg boluses. Fluid, then tryptase at 1–2 h and 24 h, and referral to an allergy clinic

Four reversible-cause lists worth keeping straight

  • 4 Hs: hypoxia, hypovolaemia, hypo/hyperkalaemia and metabolic, hypothermia.
  • 4 Ts: thrombosis (coronary or pulmonary), tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins.
  • Anaphylaxis vs tension pneumothorax vs LAST all present as sudden intraoperative collapse — the differentiating features are rash and bronchospasm, unilateral absent breath sounds with tracheal deviation, and a recent large block, respectively.
  • Hyperkalaemia: calcium chloride or gluconate to stabilise the membrane, insulin–dextrose and salbutamol to shift, then removal by dialysis or a binder. Calcium does nothing to the potassium level itself.
