Hypothalamus, pituitary, adrenal & steroids

The hypothalamus — its hormones, other functions and diseases — then the pituitary, the adrenal gland, Cushing versus Addison versus Conn, and the steroid equivalency table.

The hypothalamus — the link between brain and hormones

A small (about 4 g) area below the thalamus, forming the floor and lower walls of the third ventricle. It sits directly above the pituitary and is joined to it by the stalk (infundibulum).

  • Two routes to the pituitary. Small (parvocellular) neurons release hormones into the median eminence, and the portal veins carry them to the anterior pituitary. Large (magnocellular) neurons of the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei send their axons straight down the stalk into the posterior pituitary.
  • Parts of the hypothalamic region sit outside the blood–brain barrier (the circumventricular organs — median eminence, OVLT, subfornical organ), so they can "taste" the blood: plasma osmolality, angiotensin II, cytokines.

Feedback — why long-term steroids matter to us

  • Each axis is controlled by negative feedback: the final hormone switches off both the hypothalamus and the pituitary. Cortisol suppresses CRH and ACTH.
  • Give exogenous steroids for long enough and CRH and ACTH stay switched off → the adrenal cortex shrinks → it cannot mount a stress response to surgery → needs perioperative steroid cover.
  • Prolactin is the only one held down rather than pushed up — everything else in the anterior pituitary falls when the hypothalamus is cut off.

Diabetes insipidus — the two exam twists

  • Central or nephrogenic? Give desmopressin: central DI concentrates the urine; nephrogenic DI does not, because the kidney cannot respond. Nephrogenic causes: lithium, high calcium, low potassium, demeclocycline.
  • The triphasic response after pituitary surgery: DI first (days 1–5, the damaged nerves stop working) → SIADH (about days 5–10, the dying nerve endings leak their stored ADH) → permanent DI if enough cells are lost. Sodium can swing from high to low to high.

A neural downgrowth. Both are made in the hypothalamus (supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei) and transported down axons.

Consequence of the two different origins

  • Stalk section causes anterior pituitary failure but a RISE in prolactin — because prolactin is the only anterior hormone under tonic inhibition (by dopamine).
  • Posterior hormones are still made in the hypothalamus, so a lesion must be high to abolish ADH — hence diabetes insipidus after pituitary surgery or head injury.

What increases ADH

  • Physiological: rise in plasma osmolality (the primary stimulus, threshold about 280 mOsm/kg) · fall in blood volume or pressure via atrial and arterial baroreceptors.
  • Perioperative: surgical stress · pain · anxiety · nausea · positive pressure ventilation · raised PaCO₂ · hypoxia · exercise.
  • Drugs: morphine · nicotine · histamine · carbamazepine · SSRIs · cyclophosphamide.
  • Decreased by: alcohol · a rise in blood volume or atrial stretch (via ANP) · cold · hypo-osmolality.
  • Surgery is a potent ADH stimulus — one of the reasons postoperative patients retain water and become hyponatraemic on hypotonic fluid.

Where the lesion is — ACTH tells you

  • Cushing's with ↑ ACTH → pituitary (Cushing's disease) or ectopic ACTH.
  • Cushing's with ↓ ACTH → adrenal source, or exogenous steroid (much the commonest cause overall).
  • Addison's with ↑ ACTH → primary adrenal failure, and therefore HYPERPIGMENTATION (ACTH shares a precursor with MSH).
  • Addison's with ↓ ACTH → secondary, pituitary in origin, and NO hyperpigmentation. Aldosterone is largely preserved, so hyperkalaemia is less marked.

Addisonian crisis

  • Fever · abdominal pain · vomiting · hypotension · hypovolaemia · shock, with hypoglycaemia.
  • Precipitated by surgery, sepsis, or abrupt withdrawal of long-term steroid.
  • Treatment: hydrocortisone 100 mg IV and aggressive fluid. Do not wait for a cortisol result to treat.

Conn's syndrome

  • Primary hyperaldosteronism — adenoma or bilateral hyperplasia.
  • ↑ Na⁺ · ↑ BP · ↓ K⁺ · ↓ H⁺ → hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis.
  • Primary → renin is SUPPRESSED (low renin, high aldosterone). Secondary → renin is HIGH (renal artery stenosis, heart failure, cirrhosis, diuretics).
  • The aldosterone-to-renin ratio is the screening test. Treat with spironolactone or eplerenone, or adrenalectomy.
  • Anaesthetic relevance: correct potassium and blood pressure preoperatively; expect resistance to neuromuscular blockade reversal if profoundly hypokalaemic.

The mnemonic

  • D-1, M-4, P-5, H-20, Cortisone-25 — dexamethasone 1 (strictly 0.75), methylprednisolone 4, prednisolone 5, hydrocortisone 20, cortisone acetate 25.
  • Dexamethasone has NO mineralocorticoid activity — which is why it is the choice for cerebral oedema and as an antiemetic, and why it is useless as replacement in Addison's.
  • Fludrocortisone is the opposite: chosen entirely for its mineralocorticoid effect.
  • Hydrocortisone is 1:1 — the reference against which the others are measured, and the correct drug in an adrenal crisis.

A caveat on your table

  • The upper chart on your page gives hydrocortisone an equivalent dose of 25 mg, while the lower table gives 20 mg. 20 mg is the standard figure, and it is the one consistent with dexamethasone 0.75 mg and prednisolone 5 mg. The 25 belongs to cortisone acetate.
