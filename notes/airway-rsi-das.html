Airway: assessment, RSI and the DAS plans

Prediction, rapid sequence induction, the four DAS plans, and the two emergencies that kill — CICO and the unrecognised oesophageal intubation.

Rapid sequence induction

The purpose is a short, controlled interval between loss of consciousness and a protected airway in a patient at risk of aspiration.

  1. 1

    Preoxygenate

    To an end-tidal oxygen ≥ 0.87 to 0.9 — that is the endpoint, not three minutes by the clock, and not the saturation, which reads 100% long before the lungs are denitrogenated. 20 to 25° head up, or ramped in the obese, improves FRC and the view.

  2. 2

    Apnoeic oxygenation

    High-flow nasal oxygen through the apnoeic period extends safe apnoea time substantially. It is oxygenation, not ventilation — carbon dioxide still rises.

  3. 3

    Induce and paralyse

    A predetermined dose of induction agent with suxamethonium 1 to 1.5 mg/kg or rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg. Rocuronium at that dose gives comparable intubating conditions at 60 s and is reversible with sugammadex 16 mg/kg.

  4. 4

    Cricoid force

    10 N awake, 30 N after loss of consciousness. Release it if it worsens the view or obstructs ventilation. Evidence of benefit is weak and it is omitted in some guidelines — but you must know the numbers.

  5. 5

    Confirm

    Sustained capnography trace is the standard. "No trace = wrong place" — in cardiac arrest a flat trace may be low output, so confirm with a second method, but never assume.

Modern RSI — what has changed

  • Gentle mask ventilation is now permitted during the apnoeic period if saturations fall. Rigid "no ventilation" doctrine caused avoidable hypoxia.
  • Suxamethonium raises serum potassium by about 0.5 mmol/L normally, and catastrophically in burns beyond 24 to 48 h, denervation, spinal cord injury, prolonged immobility and neuromuscular disease.
  • Suxamethonium also causes bradycardia (especially a repeat dose in children), raised intraocular and intragastric pressure, myalgia, and masseter spasm. It is the trigger, with volatiles, for malignant hyperthermia.
  • Suxamethonium apnoea — plasma cholinesterase deficiency. Keep sedated and ventilated; test later with the dibucaine number (low number = abnormal enzyme) and screen the family.

Why prediction disappoints

  • Every bedside test, used alone, has low sensitivity and a poor positive predictive value, because difficult intubation is uncommon. Combinations are better; none is sufficient.
  • The right conclusion is not to abandon assessment but to plan for failure every time — which is precisely what NAP4 found was missing.
  • Anticipate difficult mask ventilation separately: beard, obesity, no teeth, elderly, snoring/OSA — the mnemonic BONES, plus Mallampati and male sex.
  • Previous difficulty is the single most valuable piece of information, so documentation and a patient-held alert card matter enormously.

The DAS unanticipated difficult intubation plans

  1. A

    Plan A — laryngoscopy and tracheal intubation

    Maximum three attempts, plus one by a more experienced colleague. Optimise once only: head position, external laryngeal manipulation, videolaryngoscope, bougie, release cricoid. Declare failure out loud.

  2. B

    Plan B — supraglottic airway rescue

    A second-generation device, maximum three attempts. Then stop and think: wake the patient, proceed on the SGA, intubate through it with a fibrescope, or go to tracheostomy. That explicit pause is the heart of the 2015 guideline.

  3. C

    Plan C — face-mask ventilation and wake up

    Two-person technique, oral and nasal airways, full relaxation. If oxygenation is adequate, waking the patient is a success, not a failure.

  4. D

    Plan D — CICO: emergency front-of-neck access

    Declare "cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate". Full neuromuscular blockade, 100% oxygen, head extended. DAS recommends scalpel cricothyroidotomy: scalpel, bougie, size 6.0 cuffed tube.

Scalpel cricothyroidotomy — the details examined

  • Laryngeal handshake to identify the cricothyroid membrane. Palpable membrane → transverse stab, rotate blade 90° so the sharp edge is caudad, bougie, railroad a 6.0 cuffed tube, inflate, ventilate, confirm with capnography.
  • Impalpable landmarks (obesity, neck mass, radiotherapy) → 8 to 10 cm vertical midline incision, blunt finger dissection, then as above.
  • Cannula techniques need a high-pressure jet source, are prone to kinking and barotrauma, and depend on an unobstructed upper airway for expiration. Scalpel technique is first-line because it is more reliably successful.
  • The commonest reason front-of-neck access fails is that it is started too late. Saturation has already been low for minutes while plans A to C are repeated.

The awake option and extubation

  • Awake tracheal intubation — flexible scope or videolaryngoscope — remains the technique of choice for the known difficult airway, especially with airway obstruction or an unstable cervical spine. Topical lidocaine with a stated maximum (commonly up to 9 mg/kg for airway topicalisation), minimal sedation, oxygen throughout.
  • Extubation is a planned procedure with its own DAS guideline, stratified low-risk vs at-risk, with options of an airway exchange catheter, remifentanil extubation, or deferring to a tracheostomy. A large share of airway complications happen at emergence and in recovery, not at induction.
  • Capnography must be available and used wherever a patient is intubated — theatre, ICU, the emergency department and during transfer. Its absence was a repeated contributor to death in NAP4.
