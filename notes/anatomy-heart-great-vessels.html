Anatomy of the heart and great vessels

Surface markings, chambers and valves, the coronary and conducting anatomy, the pericardium, and the vessels used for central access.

Which chamber faces where

  • Anterior (sternocostal) surface — mostly the RIGHT VENTRICLE. This is why penetrating anterior chest trauma injures the right ventricle, and why the right ventricle is the chamber compressed first in tamponade.
  • Inferior (diaphragmatic) surface — left ventricle and part of the right ventricle.
  • Posterior surface / base — the LEFT ATRIUM. It lies against the oesophagus, which is the whole basis of transoesophageal echocardiography, and the reason an enlarged left atrium causes dysphagia and compresses the left recurrent laryngeal nerve.
  • Right border — right atrium. Left border — left ventricle and left atrial appendage.

Chambers and valves

  • Right atrium — receives SVC, IVC and coronary sinus. Divided into a smooth posterior part (sinus venarum) and a trabeculated anterior part with the appendage, separated by the crista terminalis. The fossa ovalis sits on the interatrial septum; a patent foramen ovale persists in about 25 to 30% of adults and permits paradoxical embolism.
  • Right ventricle — thin-walled, crescentic in section, wrapping around the left ventricle. Coarse trabeculae, the moderator band, and a smooth outflow infundibulum. Because it is thin and crescentic it is a volume pump, poorly tolerant of acute afterload — hence its failure in pulmonary embolism and pulmonary hypertension.
  • Left atrium — four pulmonary veins, a small finger-like appendage which is the usual site of thrombus in atrial fibrillation, and the thinnest myocardium of the four chambers.
  • Left ventricle — thick-walled, conical, with fine trabeculae and two papillary muscles (anterolateral and posteromedial) supporting the mitral valve. Wall thickness roughly three times that of the right ventricle.
  • Valves: mitral is bicuspid with anterior and posterior leaflets; tricuspid has anterior, posterior and septal leaflets; aortic and pulmonary are semilunar with three cusps. The aortic cusps are named right, left and posterior (non-coronary), and the right and left coronary arteries arise from the sinuses above the right and left cusps.
  • All four valves lie in one fibrous plane — the fibrous skeleton, which electrically insulates atria from ventricles and anchors the valves. The only normal electrical connection across it is the bundle of His.
  • The posteromedial papillary muscle has a single blood supply (usually from the posterior descending artery) while the anterolateral has a dual supply — which is why inferior infarction causes papillary muscle rupture and acute mitral regurgitation far more often.

Coronary anatomy in practice

  • The coronary arteries fill in DIASTOLE — especially the left, where systolic intramyocardial pressure nearly abolishes flow. So tachycardia, which shortens diastole, and a low diastolic pressure both threaten the left ventricle. This is the physiological basis of the intra-aortic balloon pump.
  • The right ventricle is perfused throughout the cardiac cycle at normal pressures, because its wall tension is low — unless pulmonary hypertension raises it, at which point the RV becomes diastole-dependent too.
  • The left main stem is short (under 1 cm commonly) and arises from the left posterior aortic sinus; the right arises from the anterior sinus.
  • Venous drainage: the great, middle and small cardiac veins drain to the coronary sinus, which empties into the right atrium — the route for retrograde cardioplegia. Anterior cardiac veins drain the right ventricle directly, and the venae cordis minimae (Thebesian veins) drain straight into the chambers, contributing a small true anatomical shunt.

Conducting system and innervation

  • SA node — at the junction of the SVC and right atrium, at the upper end of the crista terminalis. The intrinsic rate is about 100 to 110/min, reduced to a resting 60 to 80 by vagal tone — which is why atropine or a transplanted (denervated) heart runs fast.
  • AV node — in the interatrial septum at the apex of the triangle of Koch (bounded by the tricuspid annulus, the tendon of Todaro and the coronary sinus ostium). It is the only normal electrical route to the ventricles, and its delay allows atrial filling.
  • Bundle of His → right and left bundles → fascicles → Purkinje network. Intrinsic rates fall as you descend: AV junction 40 to 60, ventricle 20 to 40 — the basis of escape rhythms.
  • Sympathetic supply — T1 to T4 (to T5) via the cardiac plexus: β₁ effects raising rate, conduction and contractility. Cardiac pain refers to T1–T4 dermatomes: chest, left arm, jaw — the anatomical explanation for referred angina, and the reason a high thoracic epidural or stellate ganglion block can relieve refractory angina.
  • Parasympathetic supply — vagus, predominantly to the SA and AV nodes and atria, with little ventricular innervation. Hence vagal manoeuvres slow rate and conduction but barely affect ventricular contractility.
  • Pericardium — fibrous outer layer attached to the diaphragm and sternum; serous inner layer with parietal and visceral (epicardial) layers and about 25 to 50 mL of fluid between. It is innervated by the phrenic nerve (C3,4,5), so pericardial pain refers to the shoulder tip and the diaphragm.
  • Because the fibrous pericardium is stiff, a small volume accumulating rapidly causes tamponade while a litre accumulating slowly may not — the compliance point behind Beck's triad, pulsus paradoxus and electrical alternans.

Great vessels and central venous access

VesselAnatomy and practical point
Aortic archBegins and ends at the level of the sternal angle (T4/5). Three branches: brachiocephalic, left common carotid, left subclavian. The left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks under the arch distal to the ligamentum arteriosum — hence hoarseness from an arch aneurysm, and from a large left atrium (Ortner's syndrome)
Descending aortaPasses the diaphragm at T12. Gives intercostal and segmental spinal arteries, including the artery of Adamkiewicz (usually left, T9 to L2)
Pulmonary arteryBifurcates below the arch; the ligamentum arteriosum connects the left pulmonary artery to the arch. The PA catheter path is RA → tricuspid → RV → pulmonary valve → PA, with characteristic waveform and depth changes
Internal jugular veinRuns lateral to the carotid artery within the carotid sheath, under the medial border of the sternocleidomastoid, becoming more anterior and overlapping the artery lower down. Right side preferred: straighter course, larger, avoids the thoracic duct, and the pleural dome is lower
Subclavian veinCrosses the first rib anterior to the scalenus anterior, with the artery and brachial plexus posterior to that muscle. Lower infection rate, more comfortable — but the highest pneumothorax risk and it is not compressible, so it is avoided in coagulopathy
Femoral veinMedial to the artery in the femoral triangle — NAVY / NAVEL from lateral to medial: Nerve, Artery, Vein, then the canal containing lymphatics. Quick and compressible, but the highest infection and thrombosis rates
Tip positionThe lower third of the SVC, above the pericardial reflection — at or just above the carina on a chest radiograph. A tip inside the right atrium risks perforation and tamponade and arrhythmia

Access complications that are anatomical

  • Carotid puncture — the artery lies posteromedial to the vein, so a steep or medial needle finds it. Ultrasound, a short-axis then long-axis view, and seeing the needle tip at all times.
  • Pneumothorax and haemothorax — the pleural dome rises above the clavicle, and higher on the left.
  • Thoracic duct injury — it enters the junction of the left internal jugular and subclavian veins, which is one reason the right side is preferred.
  • Nerve injury — phrenic (ipsilateral diaphragm), vagus, recurrent laryngeal, stellate ganglion (Horner's), and the brachial plexus with a subclavian approach.
  • Air embolism — head-down positioning during insertion and removal, and occlusive dressings, because a negative intrathoracic pressure will draw air in through an open hub.
