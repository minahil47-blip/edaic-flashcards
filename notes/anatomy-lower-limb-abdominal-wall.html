Lower limb nerves and the abdominal wall

The lumbar and sacral plexuses, what each nerve supplies, the abdominal wall in layers, and the block anatomy that follows from both.

The lumbar plexus — L1 to L4

Formed within the psoas major muscle from the anterior rami of L1 to L4, with a contribution from T12.

Three facts that are repeatedly examined

  • A femoral nerve block paralyses the quadriceps and creates a fall risk — which is exactly why the adductor canal block has displaced it for knee arthroplasty: it catches the saphenous nerve and the nerve to vastus medialis while largely sparing quadriceps strength.
  • Obturator nerve block is needed for complete anaesthesia of the knee and for TURBT of a lateral bladder wall tumour — stimulation of the nerve by the resectoscope causes a violent adductor jerk and bladder perforation. A "3-in-1" femoral block does not reliably reach the obturator nerve.
  • The hip joint is supplied by the femoral, obturator and sciatic nerves (and the nerve to quadratus femoris) — so hip pain is never fully abolished by a single peripheral block, and a fascia iliaca or femoral nerve block gives good but incomplete analgesia for a fractured neck of femur.

Block anatomy, from the anatomy

  • Femoral nerve block — at the inguinal crease, the nerve lies lateral to the femoral artery, deep to fascia iliaca and superficial to iliopsoas. Remember NAVY from lateral to medial: Nerve, Artery, Vein, then the canal (lymphatics). The artery lies at the mid-inguinal point — midway between the pubic symphysis and the anterior superior iliac spine — while the midpoint of the inguinal ligament is a different landmark (midway ASIS to pubic tubercle) and marks the deep inguinal ring.
  • Fascia iliaca block — deposits local anaesthetic under the fascia iliaca, aiming to reach femoral, lateral cutaneous and sometimes obturator nerves. It works because they all lie beneath that fascial plane at some point.
  • Adductor canal (subsartorial) block — the canal runs from the apex of the femoral triangle to the adductor hiatus, bounded by sartorius anteriorly, vastus medialis anterolaterally and adductor longus/magnus posteriorly. It contains the femoral artery and vein, the saphenous nerve and the nerve to vastus medialis.
  • Lumbar plexus (psoas compartment) block — deep, with risks of epidural or intrathecal spread, retroperitoneal haematoma and renal puncture. Treated as a deep block for anticoagulation purposes.

The sacral plexus — L4 to S4

  • Sciatic nerve (L4–S3) — the largest nerve in the body. Leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen below piriformis, passes midway between the ischial tuberosity and the greater trochanter, and runs down the posterior thigh. It supplies the hamstrings and everything below the knee except the saphenous territory.
  • It divides into tibial and common peroneal nerves, usually in the lower thigh but with a variable level — which is why the popliteal block must be placed proximal to the divergence, visible on ultrasound as the two nerves separating.
  • Tibial nerve — posterior compartment, plantar flexion and inversion; continues behind the medial malleolus and divides into the medial and lateral plantar nerves supplying the sole.
  • Common peroneal nerve — wraps around the neck of the fibula, where it is superficial and vulnerable: this is the classic site of compression injury from lithotomy or lateral position, or a tight plaster, causing foot drop and loss of sensation over the dorsum. It divides into deep peroneal (dorsiflexion; the web space between the first and second toes) and superficial peroneal (eversion; dorsum of the foot).
  • Posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh (S1–S3), superior and inferior gluteal nerves, and the pudendal nerve (S2–S4) — the last supplying the perineum, external anal and urethral sphincters; blocked at the ischial spine for obstetric and perineal procedures.
  • The ankle block needs five nerves: posterior tibial (behind the medial malleolus, posterior to the artery), saphenous (anterior to the medial malleolus, with the long saphenous vein), deep peroneal (lateral to the anterior tibial artery between the tendons), superficial peroneal (subcutaneous, anterolateral), and sural (behind the lateral malleolus). Note that one of the five — the saphenous — is a femoral branch, not sciatic, which is why a popliteal block alone leaves the medial foot unblocked.

Dermatomes, myotomes and reflexes — the landmarks

  • Dermatomes: L1 groin · L2 anterior thigh · L3 knee · L4 medial malleolus and medial leg · L5 dorsum of the foot and the great toe · S1 lateral foot, little toe, sole · S2–S4 perineum (saddle).
  • Myotomes: L2–L3 hip flexion · L3–L4 knee extension · L4–L5 dorsiflexion · L5–S1 hip extension and knee flexion · S1–S2 plantar flexion.
  • Reflexes: knee jerk L3–L4 · ankle jerk S1–S2 · anal reflex S4–S5. Upper limb for comparison: biceps C5–C6, triceps C7.
  • For surgical anaesthesia: T10 for TURP and for lower limb tourniquet discomfort, L1–L2 for the thigh, S2–S4 for perineal surgery. A block adequate for the skin incision may be inadequate for traction on the peritoneum or for a tourniquet.

Nerve supply and what it means for blocks

  • The anterior abdominal wall is supplied by T7 to L1 — the thoracoabdominal nerves (T7–T11), subcostal (T12), and iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal (L1). These run in the plane between internal oblique and transversus abdominis, which is precisely why that plane is the target.
  • Landmarks: T7 at the xiphisternum, T10 at the umbilicus, L1 at the inguinal ligament.
  • A TAP block covers the SOMATIC wall only. It gives no visceral analgesia, so it is always an adjunct to a multimodal regimen rather than a substitute for it.
  • A posterior TAP approach covers roughly T10 to L1 — adequate for a lower-abdominal transverse incision but not for an upper-abdominal or midline incision crossing the midline, which needs bilateral blocks and higher coverage. Subcostal TAP is used for upper abdominal surgery.
  • Rectus sheath block — local anaesthetic deposited between the rectus muscle and the posterior sheath, where the terminal branches of T9–T11 run before they pierce the muscle. It covers a midline incision, which a TAP does not reach well.
  • Quadratus lumborum block deposits anaesthetic around that muscle, posterior to the TAP; spread into the thoracolumbar fascia and sometimes the paravertebral space gives a wider and more posterior block, and some visceral effect.
  • Large volumes are used in all these blocks, so the plasma concentration is high — dose must be calculated on weight, and these blocks are a recognised cause of LAST (see LAST).

The inguinal region

  • Inguinal canal — about 4 cm, running from the deep ring (a defect in transversalis fascia at the midpoint of the inguinal ligament) to the superficial ring (a defect in the external oblique aponeurosis above and medial to the pubic tubercle).
  • Walls: anterior external oblique aponeurosis (plus internal oblique laterally) · posterior transversalis fascia (plus the conjoint tendon medially) · roof arching fibres of internal oblique and transversus · floor the inguinal ligament with the lacunar ligament medially.
  • Contents: the spermatic cord (or round ligament) and the ilioinguinal nerve. The cord contains three arteries (testicular, cremasteric, artery to the vas), three nerves (genital branch of genitofemoral, ilioinguinal, sympathetics), three other structures (vas deferens, pampiniform plexus, lymphatics) and three fascial coverings.
  • Hernias — the key discriminator: an indirect hernia passes through the deep ring, lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels, and follows the canal; a direct hernia pushes through the posterior wall medial to the inferior epigastric vessels. A femoral hernia lies below and lateral to the pubic tubercle, whereas an inguinal hernia lies above and medial to it — and femoral hernias strangulate far more often because the femoral ring is narrow and rigid.
  • Hesselbach's triangle — bounded by the inguinal ligament, the inferior epigastric vessels and the rectus abdominis — is the site of a direct hernia.
  • Anaesthesia for hernia repair: ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric block, field infiltration, TAP, spinal or general anaesthesia. Chronic post-herniorrhaphy groin pain affects a significant minority and is usually from injury to one of these nerves — an important example of chronic post-surgical pain (see Chronic pain).
