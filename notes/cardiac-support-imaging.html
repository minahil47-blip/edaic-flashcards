CPB, mechanical support, TOE and congenital heart disease

The bypass circuit and its physiology, counterpulsation and ECMO, enough echocardiography to answer the questions, and the congenital lesions an adult anaesthetist now meets routinely.

Cardiopulmonary bypass

Circuit: venous reservoir → oxygenator with integral heat exchanger → arterial pump → arterial line filter, plus cardiotomy suction, cardioplegia line and a vent. Gravity drainage from the right atrium, return to the ascending aorta.

Physiology on bypass

  • Hypothermia halves oxygen consumption for roughly every 10 °C fall (the Q₁₀ effect), which is what permits low flow and, at 18 °C, about 30 to 40 minutes of circulatory arrest.
  • Haemodilution offsets the rise in viscosity with cooling, at the cost of oxygen-carrying capacity and dilution of clotting factors.
  • Alpha-stat vs pH-stat: alpha-stat accepts the alkaline drift of cooling and keeps values normal when measured at 37 °C. pH-stat adds CO₂ to hold pH 7.4 at the actual temperature — raising cerebral blood flow but also embolic load. Only pH-stat involves adding CO₂.
  • Pulse oximetry, NIBP and capnography all fail on full bypass — no pulsatile flow, no ventilation. Monitoring becomes line pressures, ACT, arterial and venous temperatures, in-line oxygenator saturation and cerebral oximetry.
  • SIRS from contact activation of complement, kallikrein and neutrophils on the circuit surfaces — the origin of post-bypass vasodilatation, oedema and coagulopathy.

Cardioplegia and weaning

  • Cardioplegia arrests the heart in DIASTOLE — a flaccid heart with minimal oxygen consumption. Cold hyperkalaemic depolarising arrest, as crystalloid or blood cardioplegia, with del Nido and Custodiol as single-dose alternatives. A contracted, arrested heart ("stone heart") is ischaemic injury, not successful cardioplegia.
  • Antegrade into the aortic root or the ostia; retrograde via the coronary sinus when aortic regurgitation prevents root delivery or severe coronary stenosis would leave territory unprotected.
  • Aortic regurgitation causes LV distension during antegrade delivery — the solution regurgitates into the ventricle instead of perfusing the coronaries. A vent and retrograde delivery are the answer.
  • Weaning checklist: rewarmed, rhythm, electrolytes, haematocrit, de-aired under TOE, lungs re-expanded and ventilation restarted before separation, vasoactive drugs ready, TOE on ventricular function.
  • Protamine has three reactions: dose-related hypotension from rapid injection, true anaphylaxis, and catastrophic pulmonary vasoconstriction with right ventricular failure. Give it slowly, with the surgeon warned.
  • Tranexamic acid reduces blood loss; high doses carry a dose-related seizure risk, so lower-dose regimens are now preferred.

Intra-aortic balloon pump

  1. 1

    Timing — the whole exam point

    Inflates in DIASTOLE at the dicrotic notch; deflates just before systole. Counterpulsation. Inflating in systole would obstruct ejection and raise afterload catastrophically.

  2. 2

    Why it works

    Diastolic inflation raises coronary perfusion pressure; pre-systolic deflation reduces afterload and myocardial work. Supply up and demand down in one device — rare. Cardiac output rises modestly, by about 0.5 to 1 L/min.

  3. 3

    Position

    Descending aorta just distal to the left subclavian, above the renal arteries — about the level of the carina, or the second to third intercostal space on a chest film. Too high risks subclavian and cerebral embolism; too low the renal and mesenteric vessels.

  4. 4

    Contraindications and complications

    Aortic regurgitation (diastolic inflation worsens the regurgitant volume), aortic dissection, aneurysm or stent graft, severe peripheral vascular disease. Complications: limb ischaemia, thrombocytopenia, haemolysis, balloon rupture, dissection, infection.

Two details that get asked

  • Helium inflates the balloon — low density allows rapid shuttling at high heart rates, and it is relatively rapidly absorbed if the balloon ruptures. Blood in the gas line means rupture: remove immediately.
  • IABP-SHOCK II found no mortality benefit in infarct-related cardiogenic shock, which is why the device was downgraded from routine use to selected indications.

Differential hypoxia — the harlequin

  • In peripheral femoral VA ECMO, poorly oxygenated native output perfuses the aortic arch while well-oxygenated circuit return perfuses the lower body: the upper body desaturates while the legs are pink.
  • So monitor the right radial artery, and manage by improving native lung function, or converting to a veno-arterial-venous configuration with a return limb to the internal jugular vein.

The rest of ECMO

  • ECMO is purely supportive — a bridge to recovery, transplantation, a durable device, or a decision. It does not treat the lung or heart.
  • Bleeding is the commonest complication: systemic anticoagulation, platelet consumption, and acquired von Willebrand syndrome from high shear cleaving large multimers. Intracranial haemorrhage carries the worst outcome.
  • Anticoagulation with unfractionated heparin by ACT or anti-Xa, at lower targets than cardiopulmonary bypass; bivalirudin or argatroban in heparin-induced thrombocytopenia.
  • Ultra-protective "lung rest" ventilation once established — very low tidal volumes and driving pressures, since the circuit handles gas exchange.
  • CESAR and EOLIA are the respiratory trials; ECPR is used in selected refractory arrest with a witnessed arrest, short low-flow time and a reversible cause.
  • Femoral arterial cannulation needs a distal perfusion cannula and regular limb assessment.

Doppler, in the form that is examined

  • Continuous wave: no range resolution — it records the highest velocity anywhere along the beam, but does not alias. Pulsed wave: localises depth, but aliases above the Nyquist limit (half the pulse repetition frequency).
  • Colour Doppler aliasing is not pathology — it just means the velocity exceeded the set scale.
  • Angle matters: measured velocity = true velocity × cos θ, so beyond about 20° gradients are falsely low.
  • Simplified Bernoulli: ΔP = 4v². Continuity equation for valve area: LVOT area × LVOT VTI ÷ aortic VTI — less flow-dependent than the gradient, and essential in low-flow states.
  • Severe aortic stenosis: mean gradient > 40 mmHg, peak velocity > 4 m/s, area < 1.0 cm². Low-flow low-gradient AS looks mild because the ventricle cannot generate a gradient — use area, dimensionless index and dobutamine stress.
  • TOE is MORE sensitive than TTE for aortic dissection, and is the test of choice for left atrial appendage thrombus and endocarditis. Its blind spot is the distal ascending aorta and arch behind the trachea.
  • A new regional wall motion abnormality precedes ECG change in the ischaemic cascade. Confirm any single-view finding in a second orthogonal view — shadowing, reverberation, side-lobe and mirror artefacts are common.

The cross-cutting rules

  • A right-to-left shunt speeds intravenous induction and slows inhalational induction. A left-to-right shunt has little clinically important effect on either.
  • Air filters and meticulous de-airing in any right-to-left shunt, including a patent foramen ovale under reversing conditions — a small volume of air can cause paradoxical cerebral or coronary embolism.
  • Cyanotic children are polycythaemic. A normal haemoglobin in a chronically cyanotic child is relative anaemia and suggests iron deficiency — microcytes worsen viscosity too.
  • Prolonged fasting is hazardous in cyanotic and Fontan patients, who depend on preload and low viscosity. Encourage clear fluids and start intravenous fluids early.
  • Syndromes: Down — AVSD, subglottic stenosis, atlanto-axial instability, OSA, bradycardia on gas induction, smaller tube than predicted. 22q11 / DiGeorge — conotruncal anomalies, hypocalcaemia, thymic aplasia needing irradiated blood, difficult airway.
  • Adults with congenital heart disease now outnumber children with it, so general anaesthetists meet repaired and palliated circulations routinely. Late arrhythmia, ventricular failure and conduit or valve failure are the common problems.
