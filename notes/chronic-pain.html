Chronic pain: chronification, mechanisms and interventions

How acute pain becomes chronic, the IASP classification, the drugs that actually work in neuropathic pain, and the interventional and neuromodulation techniques.

Definitions and classification

TermDefinition
Pain (IASP, 2020)An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage. The revision matters: pain does not require tissue damage, and verbal report is not required to have pain
Chronic painPain persisting or recurring for more than 3 months — and in ICD-11 it is a diagnosis in its own right, not merely a symptom
NociceptiveArising from actual or threatened damage to non-neural tissue, by activation of nociceptors
NeuropathicCaused by a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system. Burning, shooting, electric; with allodynia and hyperalgesia
NociplasticArising from altered nociception without clear evidence of tissue damage or of a nervous system lesion. The newest of the three, and the category into which fibromyalgia and much chronic widespread pain falls

The vocabulary of sensory change

  • Allodynia — pain from a stimulus that is not normally painful (light touch, a breeze).
  • Hyperalgesia — increased pain from a stimulus that normally hurts.
  • Hyperpathia, dysaesthesia (unpleasant abnormal sensation), paraesthesia (abnormal but not unpleasant), hypoalgesia and anaesthesia dolorosa (pain in an anaesthetic area).
  • Primary hyperalgesia is at the site of injury, from peripheral sensitisation. Secondary hyperalgesia extends into surrounding undamaged tissue and is evidence of central sensitisation.

Chronification — how acute becomes chronic

  1. 1

    Peripheral sensitisation

    The inflammatory soup — bradykinin, prostaglandins, substance P, histamine, serotonin, hydrogen ions, nerve growth factor — lowers nociceptor thresholds. Silent nociceptors are recruited. This is where NSAIDs and local anaesthesia act.

  2. 2

    Central sensitisation and wind-up

    Repetitive C-fibre input causes wind-up — progressively greater dorsal horn response to an unchanged stimulus — mediated by NMDA receptor activation after magnesium block is removed by sustained depolarisation. Hence the rationale for ketamine and magnesium.

  3. 3

    Structural and functional reorganisation

    Loss of inhibitory interneurones, microglial activation, Aβ fibre sprouting into lamina II so that touch gains access to nociceptive pathways (the anatomy of allodynia), descending facilitation outweighing inhibition, and cortical remapping.

  4. 4

    Psychosocial amplification

    Catastrophising, fear-avoidance, low mood, poor sleep, and the loss of work and role. These are not epiphenomena — they independently predict transition to chronic pain and are the target of pain management programmes.

Chronic post-surgical pain

  • Incidence is high and under-recognised: of the order of 5 to 10% with significant pain after many common operations, and considerably more after thoracotomy, mastectomy, amputation, hernia repair and sternotomy.
  • Risk factors: severe preoperative pain, pre-existing chronic pain, severe acute postoperative pain, psychological vulnerability and catastrophising, nerve injury during surgery, repeat surgery, and younger age.
  • The modifiable factors are the acute pain and the nerve handling. Good multimodal analgesia, regional techniques where appropriate, and surgical nerve-sparing are the practical preventives.
  • "Pre-emptive" analgesia — timing alone — has largely failed to show benefit. "Preventive" analgesia, meaning any intervention that reduces sensitisation and whose effect outlasts the drug, is the concept that survived. Ketamine and gabapentinoids have modest evidence; routine perioperative gabapentinoids are no longer recommended because of sedation and respiratory depression, particularly with opioids.
  • Phantom limb pain affects a majority after amputation. Perioperative epidural or perineural infusion has not reliably prevented it; mirror therapy and graded motor imagery are the distinctive treatments, alongside neuropathic agents.

What does not work, and why that is examinable

  • Paracetamol and NSAIDs are largely ineffective in neuropathic pain — they target the inflammatory and nociceptive mechanism, which is not the one at fault.
  • SSRIs are poor analgesics; the noradrenergic action is what matters, hence tricyclics and SNRIs rather than SSRIs.
  • Cannabis-based medicines are not recommended for chronic pain outside specific indications, on current evidence.
  • Opioid escalation in chronic non-cancer pain is the classic trap: more drug, more hyperalgesia, more harm, no better function.

Interventional techniques

InterventionIndication and comment
Diagnostic blocksDefine the pain generator before any destructive or implanted procedure. A high placebo response makes a single uncontrolled block unreliable
Medial branch block & radiofrequency denervationFacet joint pain, after concordant diagnostic blocks. Conventional (thermal) RF at 80 °C destroys the nerve; benefit lasts months to a couple of years until the nerve regenerates
Pulsed radiofrequencyDelivered in bursts keeping tissue below about 42 °C — neuromodulatory rather than destructive, so it can be used near a mixed or motor nerve
Epidural steroid injectionRadicular pain from disc herniation; short-term benefit, no change in the need for surgery. Transforaminal, interlaminar or caudal; particulate steroid must never be injected transforaminally in the cervical region (embolic cord infarction)
Sympathetic blocksStellate ganglion (head, neck, upper limb — watch for Horner's, recurrent laryngeal and phrenic block, vertebral artery injection), lumbar sympathetic (lower limb, CRPS, ischaemic pain), coeliac plexus, superior hypogastric, ganglion impar
Trigeminal proceduresGlycerol rhizolysis, balloon compression, radiofrequency, and microvascular decompression — the only procedure that addresses the cause
Intrathecal drug deliveryRefractory cancer and selected non-cancer pain: morphine with bupivacaine and clonidine, ziconotide (an N-type calcium channel blocker) in specialist use. Risks: granuloma, infection, and catastrophic error if the pump is filled or programmed wrongly

Neuromodulation

  • Spinal cord stimulation — epidural electrodes, classically explained by the gate control theory: Aβ stimulation inhibits nociceptive transmission in the dorsal horn, though descending and supraspinal mechanisms are also involved.
  • Best evidence in failed back surgery syndrome / persistent spinal pain, CRPS and refractory angina and critical limb ischaemia. A trial of stimulation precedes permanent implantation.
  • Newer waveforms — high-frequency (10 kHz) and burst stimulation — give analgesia without paraesthesia, which also makes them hard to blind. Dorsal root ganglion stimulation suits focal pain, particularly in the groin and foot.
  • Problems: lead migration and fracture, infection, loss of efficacy over time, and MRI compatibility, which must be checked before any scan.
  • Peripheral nerve stimulation, occipital nerve stimulation for refractory headache, deep brain and motor cortex stimulation for central pain, and TENS, which is non-invasive, harmless and of modest, inconsistent benefit.

Complex regional pain syndrome

  • Budapest criteria — required for diagnosis: continuing disproportionate pain, plus symptoms in ≥ 3 of 4 categories and signs in ≥ 2 of 4: sensory (allodynia, hyperalgesia), vasomotor (temperature or colour asymmetry), sudomotor/oedema (sweating, swelling) and motor/trophic (weakness, tremor, dystonia, nail and hair change).
  • Type I has no identifiable nerve lesion; type II follows a definite nerve injury. Both are otherwise the same syndrome, and the distinction does not change treatment.
  • Treatment is early and functional: physiotherapy and desensitisation, graded motor imagery and mirror therapy, neuropathic agents, bisphosphonates, and sympathetic blocks or spinal cord stimulation if progress stalls. Immobilisation and prolonged rest make it worse.
  • Prevention: vitamin C after wrist fracture has some evidence; avoiding tight casts and encouraging early movement is standard advice.

The biopsychosocial model, and why it is the answer

  • Chronic pain is managed, not cured. The goals are function, sleep, mood, work and self-efficacy — not a pain score of zero.
  • Pain management programmes — multidisciplinary, group-based, combining education, graded activity, CBT and acceptance-based approaches — have better evidence for improving function than any single drug or procedure.
  • Assessment tools: visual analogue and numerical rating scales, the Brief Pain Inventory, the McGill Pain Questionnaire (sensory, affective and evaluative descriptors), DN4 / painDETECT / LANSS for identifying neuropathic pain, and HADS and catastrophising scales for the psychological dimension.
  • In the opioid-tolerant patient coming to surgery: continue the baseline opioid, expect much higher requirements, use regional and multimodal techniques including ketamine, involve the acute pain team, and plan the de-escalation before discharge.
