Postoperative delirium and cognitive dysfunction

Two different conditions that are constantly confused. How to tell them apart, what actually prevents delirium, and what the evidence does and does not support.

The distinctions that are examined

  • Delirium is a disturbance of ATTENTION and awareness; POCD is a measured deficit in memory and executive function with normal attention and consciousness. If you remember only one line, remember that.
  • POCD cannot be diagnosed clinically. It requires a preoperative baseline and formal testing — which is why reported incidences vary enormously and why it is a research construct more than a bedside diagnosis. The newer umbrella term is perioperative neurocognitive disorders.
  • Hypoactive delirium is commoner than hyperactive and is routinely missed, because a quiet, withdrawn patient does not attract attention. Mixed is commonest of all.
  • Emergence delirium (agitation in the first minutes after anaesthesia, classically in children after sevoflurane) is a different entity from postoperative delirium and is short-lived.
  • Delirium is not benign. It is independently associated with longer stay, more complications, loss of independence, institutionalisation, long-term cognitive decline and increased mortality.

Incidence and risk factors

  • Incidence rises steeply with age and with the magnitude of surgery: of the order of 10 to 20% after major elective surgery in the elderly, 35 to 65% after hip fracture surgery, and up to 50 to 80% in ventilated ICU patients.
  • POCD is reported in roughly 25 to 40% at one week and about 10% at three months after major non-cardiac surgery in the elderly — with the important finding from the ISPOCD studies that at three months the incidence was similar after general and regional anaesthesia.
  • Predisposing (patient) factors — the ones you cannot change: age over 65, pre-existing cognitive impairment or dementia (the strongest single predictor), frailty, depression, sensory impairment, poor functional status, alcohol excess, polypharmacy, and previous delirium.
  • Precipitating factors — the ones you can: pain, dehydration, infection, hypoxia, electrolyte and glucose derangement, constipation, urinary retention and catheters, sleep deprivation, immobility, physical restraint, an unfamiliar environment, and drugs.
  • The drugs that matter: anticholinergics (the clearest offenders — hyoscine, cyclizine, oxybutynin, amitriptyline, atropine over glycopyrronium), benzodiazepines, pethidine (norpethidine), high-dose opioids, and steroids. Use the anticholinergic burden score if you want a number.
  • Surgery itself: emergency more than elective, cardiac and hip fracture highest, long duration, major blood loss, and postoperative ICU admission.

Mechanism, briefly

  • Neuroinflammation is the leading model: surgical tissue injury releases cytokines which cross a permeable blood–brain barrier, activate microglia and disrupt synaptic function — in a brain with reduced cognitive reserve.
  • Added to this: cholinergic deficiency (which explains the anticholinergic drug association), dopaminergic excess, melatonin and circadian disruption, and impaired cerebral autoregulation.
  • The crucial implication: delirium is largely a consequence of the surgical insult and the patient's reserve, not of the anaesthetic agent — which is why agent choice turns out to matter much less than everyone expected.

Prevention — what works

  1. 1

    Multicomponent non-pharmacological care — the best evidence there is

    The HELP-style bundle: orientation and cognitive stimulation, glasses and hearing aids returned, sleep hygiene with reduced night-time noise and interruption, early mobilisation, hydration and nutrition, bowel and bladder care, early catheter removal, pain control, and avoidance of restraint. This reduces delirium incidence by roughly a third — a larger effect than any drug.

  2. 2

    Comprehensive geriatric assessment

    Proactive, pre-admission, multidisciplinary review with medication rationalisation — particularly deprescribing anticholinergics and benzodiazepines. Reduces delirium after hip fracture and major surgery.

  3. 3

    Anaesthetic management

    Avoid benzodiazepine premedication. Use regional analgesia and opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia — a fascia iliaca or femoral block for hip fracture reduces both pain and opioid dose. Maintain cerebral perfusion: avoid hypotension, hypoxia, anaemia, hypoglycaemia and hypercapnia. Keep the patient warm and normovolaemic.

  4. 4

    Depth of anaesthesia

    Processed EEG-guided anaesthesia to avoid unnecessarily deep anaesthesia and burst suppression is recommended in older patients. The evidence is mixed — some trials show reduced delirium, ENGAGES did not — but avoiding profound, unmonitored depth is sensible and harmless.

  5. 5

    In ICU: the ABCDEF bundle

    Assess and manage pain · Both spontaneous awakening and breathing trials · Choice of sedation · Delirium assess, prevent, manage · Early mobility and exercise · Family engagement. Light sedation targets (RASS 0 to −2), avoiding benzodiazepines, with propofol or dexmedetomidine instead.

What does NOT work — the negative results that get examined

  • Prophylactic antipsychotics do not prevent delirium. Haloperidol prophylaxis has repeatedly failed (REDUCE, and others), and carries QT and extrapyramidal risk.
  • Cholinesterase inhibitors do not work — a rivastigmine trial in ICU delirium was stopped early for increased mortality.
  • Regional anaesthesia does not reliably reduce delirium or POCD compared with general anaesthesia. The RAGA and REGAIN trials in hip fracture found no significant difference in delirium, and ISPOCD found no difference in POCD at three months. What matters is what you do within the technique — sedation depth, opioid load, blood pressure — not the label on it.
  • Melatonin and ramelteon — plausible and widely used, with inconsistent trial results. Reasonable as part of sleep hygiene, not as a proven preventive.
  • Dexmedetomidine has the best signal of any drug, particularly in cardiac surgery and ICU, but the evidence is not strong enough for routine prophylaxis and it causes bradycardia and hypotension.
  • There is no evidence that any anaesthetic agent causes dementia in humans. Animal work on anaesthetic neurotoxicity and the GAS, PANDA and MASK studies in children — which found no difference in neurodevelopmental outcome after brief single exposures — should be quoted rather than speculation.

Treating established delirium

  • Find and fix the cause first. The differential is a checklist, not a guess: hypoxia, hypotension, hypoglycaemia, sepsis, pain, urinary retention, constipation, drug effect or withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepine, nicotine, opioid), electrolytes and sodium, uraemia, hepatic encephalopathy, stroke, raised intracranial pressure, and Wernicke's encephalopathy — give thiamine if there is any suspicion.
  • Non-pharmacological management is first-line and remains the mainstay: reorientation, a calm familiar presence, family involvement, lighting and noise, mobilisation, and removal of catheters, lines and restraints wherever possible.
  • Drugs are for distress or danger only — when the patient is at risk of harming themselves or others, or is so distressed that essential care is impossible. They do not shorten delirium.
  • If a drug is needed: a single low dose of haloperidol (for example 0.5 mg orally or intramuscularly in the elderly) or an atypical such as olanzapine or quetiapine, reviewed daily and stopped as soon as possible. Watch the QT interval and extrapyramidal effects.
  • Avoid antipsychotics entirely in Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies — they can cause severe, sometimes fatal, deterioration. Use a benzodiazepine cautiously there instead.
  • Benzodiazepines are first-line only for alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, and in neuroleptic malignant syndrome — otherwise they make delirium worse.
  • Follow up. Document the episode, explain it to the patient and family, and arrange cognitive follow-up — because an episode of delirium is a marker of vulnerability and predicts later decline.
