Enzyme inducers & inhibitors, kinetics and porphyria

Which drugs speed up or slow down liver metabolism and why it matters to us, how drugs are cleared at a steady rate or a steady proportion, and what is safe in porphyria.

Liver enzymes in plain terms

Most drugs are broken down in the liver by the cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes. Some drugs change how much of that enzyme there is, or block it.

Enzyme INDUCEREnzyme INHIBITOR
What it doesMakes the liver build more enzymeBlocks the enzyme that is already there
SpeedSlow — days to 2 weeks to start, weeks to wear offFast — within hours to a day or two
Other drugsBroken down faster → lower levels, less effectBroken down slower → higher levels, toxicity
Classic exampleRifampicin → the pill fails, warfarin stops workingErythromycin → midazolam lasts much longer
The twistA prodrug or a drug with a toxic metabolite becomes more active or toxic — e.g. more paracetamol → NAPQIA prodrug becomes less effective — e.g. codeine and tramadol cannot be activated

Enzyme inducers

Remember them as PC BRASS.

  • Notice the overlap with porphyria: phenytoin, carbamazepine, barbiturates, rifampicin and alcohol all appear again in the "avoid" list below. Inducing the enzymes uses up haem, which switches the haem-making pathway into overdrive.

First-order vs zero-order kinetics

How fast the body clears a drug depends on whether the clearing enzymes are coping or flat out.

First-order (most drugs)Zero-order ("saturation" kinetics)
In plain wordsThe enzymes have spare capacity — the more drug there is, the faster it is clearedThe enzymes are saturated — they clear a fixed amount per hour however much is there
What is removedA constant proportion per unit timeA constant amount per unit time
Rate of eliminationProportional to concentrationFixed — independent of concentration
Half-lifeConstantNot constant — gets longer the higher the dose
GraphExponential curve (straight line on a log scale)Straight line on an ordinary scale
Steady stateReached after about 5 half-lives; dose and level rise togetherA small dose increase can cause a big jump in level → toxicity

How one drug can be both — Michaelis–Menten

  • Rate = Vmax × C ÷ (Km + C). Km is the concentration at which the enzymes run at half their maximum speed.
  • At low concentrations (well below Km) → first-order. At high concentrations (well above Km) → the enzymes saturate → zero-order.
  • So "zero-order drugs" are really drugs whose usual doses already sit near saturation.

Zero-order drugs — remember "PEA-TTH"

  • Phenytoin — at normal therapeutic levels; why its dosing needs level checks.
  • Ethanol — cleared at roughly one unit an hour whatever the amount drunk.
  • Aspirin (salicylate) — in high doses and overdose.
  • Thiopentone — after large doses or an infusion, which is why it is not given by infusion and why recovery becomes very slow.
  • Theophylline — at the upper end of its range.
  • Heparin — at higher doses its half-life lengthens.

Acute porphyria — why drugs matter

  • What it is: an inherited fault in one enzyme of the haem-making pathway. The acute types are acute intermittent porphyria (the commonest), variegate porphyria and hereditary coproporphyria.
  • Why attacks happen: anything that increases demand for haem — especially drugs that induce ALA synthase or the CYP enzymes — revs up the pathway. The building blocks (ALA and porphobilinogen) pile up before the faulty step and are toxic to nerves.
  • Non-drug triggers: fasting, dehydration, infection, stress, alcohol, smoking, menstruation and pregnancy hormones.
  • An attack: severe abdominal pain, vomiting, tachycardia and hypertension, confusion or psychosis, seizures, hyponatraemia, and a motor neuropathy that can paralyse the breathing muscles. Urine may darken on standing.

Porphyria — perioperative care

Preventing an attack

  • Minimise fasting — start a glucose infusion if the patient must be starved. Carbohydrate suppresses ALA synthase.
  • Avoid dehydration, infection, pain and stress; use safe drugs only.
  • Regional anaesthesia is fine — but examine and document any existing neuropathy first, so a new deficit is not blamed on the block.

Treating an attack

  • Stop every trigger drug.
  • Haem arginate 3 mg/kg IV daily for 4 days — switches off ALA synthase.
  • IV glucose (carbohydrate loading) if haem arginate is delayed.
  • Pain: opioids. Tachycardia and hypertension: beta blockers. Nausea: ondansetron.
  • Watch sodium and breathing — the neuropathy can need ventilation. Seizures: gabapentin, levetiracetam or magnesium rather than the usual antiepileptics.
