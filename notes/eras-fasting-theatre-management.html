Fasting, enhanced recovery and theatre management

The fasting rules and the evidence behind them, the ERAS bundle element by element, and how an operating list is actually run efficiently.

The "2 and 6" rule, and what has changed

  • Remember it as 2 hours clear fluids, 6 hours food — with 4 hours for breast milk sitting between them.
  • Prolonged fasting is harmful, not safe. It causes thirst, hunger, headache, irritability (especially in children), hypoglycaemia in the very young, hypovolaemia, hypotension on induction, and insulin resistance. Clear fluids do not increase gastric volume or lower pH — the stomach clears them exponentially with a half-time of 10 to 20 minutes.
  • Encourage clear fluids up to 2 hours before, actively, rather than permitting them. Paediatric practice has gone further, with the "1-hour clear fluid" approach adopted by many centres after studies showed no increase in aspiration.
  • Fasting is for elective surgery with a normal stomach. It is irrelevant to the patient who is obstructed, bleeding, in pain, in labour, or who has had opioids — in those cases the stomach is never assumed empty regardless of the clock, and a rapid sequence induction is used.
  • Delayed gastric emptying: pain, opioids, anxiety, diabetic gastroparesis, pregnancy (in labour), raised intracranial pressure, trauma, and GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide — now a recognised reason for prolonged solid residue, with guidance generally advising that weekly agents be withheld for a week and that a full stomach be assumed if they have not been.
  • Aspiration prophylaxis where risk is high: a proton pump inhibitor or H₂ antagonist, sodium citrate to neutralise the existing contents (used in obstetrics because it works within minutes), and metoclopramide. Antacid particulate suspensions are avoided — aspirated particles cause worse lung injury.
  • Mendelson's syndrome — the classically quoted thresholds for risk are a gastric volume above about 25 mL (0.4 mL/kg) and pH below 2.5. These figures come from animal work and are a rule of thumb, not a validated threshold, which is itself a fair point to make.

Enhanced recovery after surgery

ERAS works because it is a bundle: no single element produces the effect, and compliance with the bundle as a whole correlates with outcome. That is the key concept, not the list.

  1. Pre

    Before admission

    Patient education and expectation-setting (which independently shortens stay), prehabilitation — exercise, nutrition, psychological preparation — smoking cessation ideally 8 weeks before, alcohol reduction, anaemia correction with iron 4 to 6 weeks ahead, and optimisation of diabetes and other comorbidity.

  2. Day

    Day of surgery

    No prolonged fasting; carbohydrate loading 2 hours before (which reduces insulin resistance, thirst and hunger — omitted in diabetes and where emptying is delayed), no routine bowel preparation for colonic surgery, no sedative premedication, thromboprophylaxis, and antibiotic prophylaxis within 60 minutes of incision.

  3. Intra

    Intraoperative

    Minimally invasive surgery where possible; short-acting anaesthetic agents; goal-directed fluid therapy — neither dry nor wet; active normothermia; PONV prophylaxis by risk score; opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia with regional or local techniques; protective ventilation at 6 to 8 mL/kg PBW with PEEP; and no routine drains or nasogastric tubes.

  4. Post

    Postoperative

    Early oral intake (within hours, not days), early mobilisation on the day of surgery, early removal of catheters and lines, regular non-opioid analgesia, chewing gum and laxatives for ileus, audit of compliance, and discharge against criteria with planned follow-up.

What ERAS achieves, and the honest limits

  • Reduced length of stay and reduced complications, without an increase in readmission — consistently, across colorectal, and now orthopaedic, gynaecological, urological, hepatobiliary and even cardiac surgery.
  • The physiological rationale is attenuation of the surgical stress response: less catabolism, less insulin resistance, less sodium and water retention, less ileus (see Stress response).
  • The anaesthetist's largest contributions are fluid management, analgesia and normothermia — the three with the clearest individual evidence.
  • Honest caveats: much of the evidence is before-and-after rather than randomised, so secular improvement contributes; shorter stay is not the same as better recovery, which is why patient-reported outcomes and days alive and at home are increasingly the endpoints; and a bundle imposed without engagement achieves poor compliance and therefore little benefit.
  • Thoracic epidural remains part of the colorectal bundle in open surgery, but in laparoscopic surgery it may prolong stay through hypotension and immobility — so the bundle must be matched to the operation, not applied mechanically.

The levers that actually work

  • Preoperative assessment clinics — the largest single reducer of on-the-day cancellation, and therefore the best-evidenced efficiency intervention in the whole pathway.
  • Scheduling by predicted duration with explicit list planning, and sequencing children, diabetics and the frail early.
  • Parallel processing — block or line placement in an induction room, or a second anaesthetist — shortens turnaround but needs staffing and a safety case; it must not dilute supervision or monitoring.
  • Team briefing at the start of the list: who is in the room, the order, the equipment and implants needed, the expected difficulties and the plan for each. It improves efficiency and safety at once, and is the first part of the WHO checklist for a reason.
  • Day surgery as the default for suitable procedures, with a dedicated day-surgery unit rather than day cases on inpatient lists, which is where they get bumped.
  • Separating emergency from elective capacity — a dedicated emergency theatre (the "CEPOD" list) prevents emergencies cancelling elective work and gets urgent cases to theatre faster, which NELA showed matters for mortality.

Where efficiency and safety conflict

  • Production pressure is a recognised contributor to error — rushed checks, skipped briefings, proceeding despite a concern, and reluctance to cancel a case that should be cancelled.
  • Anaesthetists are asked to balance two legitimate duties: stewardship of a scarce public resource, and the safety of the individual patient in front of them. Where they conflict, the individual patient prevails — and the right response to pressure is to name it explicitly to the team.
  • Fatigue, handover at shift change, and unfamiliar staff are the predictable costs of running a system at maximum utilisation (see Human factors).
  • Theatre is the hospital's most expensive real estate, so the economic argument is real — but the costs that dominate are staffing and length of stay, not drugs or disposables, which is why ERAS and cancellation reduction outperform cost-cutting on consumables (see Health economics).
