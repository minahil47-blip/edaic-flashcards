Extubation, the DAS algorithm, and recovery scoring

Emergence is where a large share of airway complications happen. The DAS extubation guideline, the at-risk patient, and the scoring systems that govern discharge from recovery.

Why extubation deserves a plan

  • A substantial proportion of serious airway complications in NAP4 occurred at emergence or in recovery, not at induction — yet extubation is usually planned far less carefully.
  • The asymmetry is the point: at induction you have a cooperative, preoxygenated patient, a full team and a reversible situation. At extubation you have oedema, secretions, residual block, a partly awake patient and a dispersing team.
  • Extubation is an elective procedure and the DAS guideline frames it exactly that way — plan, prepare, perform, and provide post-extubation care.

Criteria for a routine extubation

  • Airway: no oedema, no bleeding, surgical access regained, no pack retained (checked and documented).
  • Breathing: adequate spontaneous ventilation, acceptable tidal volume and rate, SpO₂ satisfactory on a modest FiO₂, EtCO₂ not rising uncontrollably.
  • Neuromuscular function: train-of-four ratio ≥ 0.9 by quantitative monitoring. Clinical signs — head lift, hand grip, tongue depressor test — are insufficient; a TOF count of 4 by eye still permits substantial residual block.
  • Conscious level: obeying commands, or deliberately deep extubation in a selected patient.
  • Physiology: normothermic (shivering trebles oxygen consumption), haemodynamically stable, pH and electrolytes reasonable, analgesia established.

The DAS extubation algorithm

  1. 1

    Plan

    Assess the airway and the general risk factors, and stratify into LOW-RISK or AT-RISK. This single decision drives everything that follows.

  2. 2

    Prepare

    Optimise the patient and the situation: position, oxygenation, suction, reversal, equipment for reintubation, and a briefed team who know the plan and the backup.

  3. 3

    Perform — low-risk

    Preoxygenate, suction, insert a bite block, position, awake or deep extubation, positive pressure as the cuff deflates to expel secretions, then oxygen and monitoring.

  4. 4

    Perform — at-risk

    Four options: (a) postpone extubation; (b) advanced techniques — remifentanil extubation, exchange to a supraglottic airway (the Bailey manoeuvre), or an airway exchange catheter left in situ; (c) awake extubation over a device; or (d) tracheostomy.

  5. 5

    Provide post-extubation care

    Staffing, monitoring, oxygen, a clear handover of the airway risk, and documentation plus a patient alert letter if difficulty was encountered.

Awake vs deep — the trade-off

  • Awake extubation: protective reflexes restored, lower aspiration risk — but coughing, hypertension, tachycardia, raised intraocular and intracranial pressure, and strain on surgical repairs.
  • Deep extubation: smooth, avoids coughing — useful after middle-ear, ophthalmic, intracranial or airway-reconstruction surgery — but obstruction, laryngospasm and aspiration are all likelier, so it demands a skilled operator and a patient with an easy airway and a low aspiration risk.
  • Remifentanil extubation is the compromise: a low-dose infusion continued through emergence suppresses cough and haemodynamic response while the patient is awake and obeying commands.

The at-risk patient

RiskWhy, and what to do
Known difficult airwayThe airway may be worse than at induction. Consider an airway exchange catheter, or deferring extubation entirely

Restricted accessHalo, mandibular wiring, cervical fixation, head and neck flaps — removal tools must be immediately available

Post-extubation emergencies

  • Laryngospasm — remove the stimulus, 100% oxygen, CPAP with jaw thrust, pressure at Larson's point, deepen with propofol, then suxamethonium 0.1 to 1 mg/kg (4 mg/kg intramuscularly if no access).
  • Negative pressure pulmonary oedema follows vigorous inspiratory effort against an obstruction — pink frothy sputum and desaturation within minutes. Treat with oxygen and CPAP or reintubation with PEEP; it usually resolves within 24 hours.
  • Supraglottic obstruction from oedema — sit up, nebulised adrenaline, dexamethasone, heliox as a temporising measure, and early reintubation with a smaller tube rather than late.
  • Aspiration, bronchospasm, and reintubation should all be anticipated in the plan rather than improvised.

Recovery: scoring and discharge

Discharge must be criteria-led, not time-led. Two scores are examinable: Aldrete for recovery-room discharge, and PADSS for discharge home.

ActivityMoves four limbs / two limbs / none voluntarily or on command
RespirationBreathes deeply and coughs freely / dyspnoeic or limited / apnoeic
CirculationBP within 20% of baseline / within 20–50% / beyond 50%
ConsciousnessFully awake / rousable on calling / not responding
Oxygen saturation> 92% on air / needs oxygen to hold > 90% / < 90% with oxygen

The numbers

  • Aldrete: five domains, 0 to 2 each, maximum 10. A score of 9 or more permits discharge from recovery. The modified Aldrete replaced colour with oxygen saturation — that substitution is itself a favourite question.
  • PADSS (post-anaesthetic discharge scoring system) governs discharge home, and is different: vital signs, ambulation, nausea and vomiting, pain, and surgical bleeding — five domains, 0 to 2 each, score ≥ 9 required.
  • Drinking and voiding are no longer mandatory for every day-case patient — they are required only where the procedure or the technique makes retention likely.
  • Discharge home also requires a responsible adult escort, written instructions, a contact number, and no driving, machinery, alcohol or important decisions for 24 hours.

Standards for the recovery area

  • One-to-one nursing until the patient can maintain their own airway, then no fewer than one nurse to two patients. The anaesthetist retains responsibility until a formal handover is accepted.
  • Monitoring: oxygen saturation, blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, pain and nausea scores, temperature, conscious level — and capnography must be available for any patient with an airway device or receiving sedation.
  • Handover is structured: procedure, anaesthetic technique, airway difficulty, analgesia and antiemetics given and prescribed, fluids and losses, drains, and specific instructions or limits.
  • Hypothermia delays recovery, increases bleeding, wound infection and cardiac events — active warming starts in theatre, not in recovery.
  • Common recovery problems: pain, PONV, hypotension, hypoxaemia, hypertension, agitation or emergence delirium, shivering, urinary retention, and residual neuromuscular block presenting as weakness or obstruction.
