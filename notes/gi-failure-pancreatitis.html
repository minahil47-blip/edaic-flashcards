Gastrointestinal failure: ileus, peritonitis, pancreatitis

The three non-hepatic forms of GI failure named in the syllabus — plus intra-abdominal hypertension, which ties them together.

Postoperative ileus

A transient impairment of coordinated propulsion after surgery. Small bowel motility typically returns within hours, stomach within 24 to 48 h, and colon is last at 48 to 72 h — so colonic function determines when the patient passes flatus.

Causes, and how anaesthesia contributes

  • Sympathetic overactivity and the inflammatory response to surgical handling are the two main mechanisms — hence the benefit of minimally invasive surgery and of blocking the sympathetic outflow.
  • Opioids act on enteric µ receptors and are the most important modifiable drug cause. Electrolyte disturbance — hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypocalcaemia — and fluid overload causing bowel wall oedema are the others.
  • What reduces ileus: thoracic epidural with local anaesthetic (sympathetic block, opioid sparing — and note that a thoracic epidural is required; lumbar does not have the same effect), opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia, goal-directed rather than liberal fluid therapy, early enteral feeding, early mobilisation, chewing gum, and laparoscopic surgery.
  • Nasogastric tubes do not prevent or shorten ileus and increase pulmonary complications — routine prophylactic use is abandoned. They are for symptomatic decompression.
  • Alvimopan, a peripherally acting µ antagonist, shortens ileus after bowel resection but has restricted availability.

Two named conditions

  • Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie's syndrome) — massive colonic dilatation with no mechanical cause, in the elderly, post-operative, or neurologically impaired. Perforation risk rises steeply above a caecal diameter of about 12 cm. Correct electrolytes, stop opioids and anticholinergics, decompress, and give neostigmine 2 to 2.5 mg slowly with cardiac monitoring and atropine available — bradycardia is the hazard.
  • Sigmoid volvulus — coffee-bean sign, decompressed endoscopically; recurrence is common.

Peritonitis & the emergency laparotomy

  1. 1

    Recognise the physiology

    Massive third-space sequestration into the gut lumen, wall and peritoneal cavity — the patient is profoundly hypovolaemic even without visible loss. Vomiting adds a hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis; bowel ischaemia and hypoperfusion add a lactic acidosis.

  2. 2

    Resuscitate, but do not delay

    Balanced crystalloid, antibiotics, analgesia, nasogastric decompression. Source control is the treatment — resuscitation that delays theatre in faecal peritonitis costs lives. Correct what can be corrected en route.

  3. 3

    Anaesthetise for a full stomach and a failing circulation

    Rapid sequence induction with a reduced dose and a vasopressor drawn up, invasive monitoring, large-bore access, active warming. Expect profound hypotension on induction and again on abdominal decompression.

  4. 4

    Risk-score and plan the destination

    NELA, P-POSSUM or similar, documented preoperatively. High-risk patients need consultant anaesthetist and surgeon presence and a planned critical care bed — the interventions NELA showed actually reduce mortality.

Antibiotics and the microbiology

  • Primary (spontaneous) peritonitis — no breach of the GI tract; in cirrhotic ascites, ascitic neutrophils > 250/mm³, usually monomicrobial. Treat and then give prophylaxis.
  • Secondary peritonitis — perforation or anastomotic leak; polymicrobial with coliforms, enterococci and anaerobes. Needs source control plus broad-spectrum cover.
  • Tertiary peritonitis — persistent or recurrent after apparently adequate treatment, with resistant organisms, Candida and coagulase-negative staphylococci. A marker of a poor prognosis.
  • Antibiotics do not substitute for drainage. Failure to improve usually means an uncontrolled source, not the wrong antibiotic.

Acute pancreatitis

Diagnosis requires two of three: characteristic epigastric pain radiating to the back, amylase or lipase more than three times the upper limit of normal, and characteristic imaging.

Glasgow-Imrie — PANCREAS

  • PaO₂ < 8 kPa · Age > 55 · Neutrophils (WCC > 15) · Calcium < 2 mmol/L · Renal (urea > 16 mmol/L) · Enzymes (LDH > 600, AST > 200) · Albumin < 32 g/L · Sugar (glucose > 10 mmol/L).
  • Three or more within 48 hours indicates severe pancreatitis. Note that amylase is not in the score — a point frequently tested.

Management — and what has been abandoned

  • Fluid resuscitation with balanced crystalloid, titrated to response. The WATERFALL trial showed aggressive fixed-rate fluid caused more fluid overload without benefit — so "fluid, lots of it" is now wrong.
  • Analgesia — opioids are appropriate; the old concern about morphine and sphincter of Oddi spasm has no clinical support. Thoracic epidural is used in some centres.
  • Enteral nutrition, early, is the standard — nasogastric feeding is as good as nasojejunal, and far better than parenteral nutrition, which increases infection. "Resting the pancreas" by starvation is obsolete.
  • No prophylactic antibiotics. They are given only for proven or strongly suspected infected necrosis, or for cholangitis.
  • Infected necrosis — suspect after the first week with deterioration and gas in the collection; manage by a step-up approach: percutaneous or endoscopic drainage first, then minimally invasive necrosectomy. Open necrosectomy is a last resort (PANTER trial).
  • Gallstone pancreatitis — urgent ERCP only for cholangitis or persistent biliary obstruction, and cholecystectomy during the same admission for mild disease, or deferred until collections resolve in severe disease.
  • Complications to know: necrosis, pseudocyst (after 4 weeks), haemorrhage, splenic and portal vein thrombosis, pancreatic ascites and fistula, hypocalcaemia from fat saponification, hyperglycaemia, ARDS, AKI, and intra-abdominal hypertension.

Why it causes multi-organ failure

  • Respiratory — the diaphragm is splinted: reduced FRC and compliance, raised airway pressures, hypoxaemia and hypercapnia. Measured plateau pressure overestimates the transpulmonary pressure, because much of it is spent on the chest wall.
  • Cardiovascular — reduced venous return and cardiac output, while CVP and PAOP read falsely HIGH from transmitted pressure. A classic trap: the numbers suggest overload while the patient is underfilled.
  • Renal — renal vein compression and reduced perfusion pressure cause oliguria that does not respond to fluid. Often the earliest sign.
  • Splanchnic — gut ischaemia, loss of barrier function; intracranial — raised ICP through impaired venous drainage and raised intrathoracic pressure.
  • Management: decompress the lumen (nasogastric and rectal tubes, prokinetics), drain intraperitoneal fluid percutaneously, improve abdominal wall compliance (sedation, analgesia, neuromuscular blockade, avoid head-up beyond 30°), avoid fluid overload and consider negative balance, and finally decompressive laparotomy with a temporary abdominal closure for refractory compartment syndrome.
