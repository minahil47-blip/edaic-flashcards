Hyperbaric medicine

The gas laws that make it work, the accepted indications, decompression illness, and why anaesthetising a patient in a chamber is genuinely difficult.

The gas laws, applied

LawStatementClinical consequence
Boyle'sAt constant temperature, P × V is constantA gas bubble or air space shrinks on compression and expands on decompression. This is the mechanism by which hyperbaric therapy crushes gas bubbles — and the reason barotrauma occurs on ascent
Dalton'sTotal pressure is the sum of partial pressuresAt 3 atmospheres on 100% oxygen, the inspired oxygen partial pressure is roughly three times what it is at the surface
Henry'sThe amount of gas dissolved in a liquid is proportional to its partial pressureThe key law: dissolved oxygen in plasma rises enormously, and nitrogen dissolves into tissues during a dive and must be eliminated slowly

The oxygen arithmetic worth being able to do

  • Dissolved oxygen is 0.003 mL per 100 mL of blood per mmHg of PaO₂ (about 0.023 mL/100 mL per kPa). At a normal PaO₂ of 100 mmHg that is a trivial 0.3 mL/100 mL against the 20 mL carried by haemoglobin.
  • At 3 atmospheres absolute on 100% oxygen, PaO₂ approaches 2,000 mmHg — giving roughly 6 mL of dissolved oxygen per 100 mL of plasma.
  • That is enough to meet resting tissue oxygen demand with no haemoglobin at all — the famous demonstration in exsanguinated animals, and the principle behind using hyperbaric oxygen in severe anaemia and carbon monoxide poisoning, where haemoglobin is unavailable as a carrier.
  • Depth and pressure: every 10 metres of sea water adds 1 atmosphere. So 10 m = 2 ATA, 20 m = 3 ATA. Therapeutic tables usually run at 2.0 to 3.0 ATA for 60 to 120 minutes.

How hyperbaric oxygen works

  • Bubble reduction — direct mechanical compression by Boyle's law, plus a steep diffusion gradient that washes nitrogen out of the bubble. The basis of treating decompression illness and arterial gas embolism.
  • Displacement of carbon monoxide — high oxygen partial pressure accelerates dissociation of carboxyhaemoglobin, reducing its half-life from about 4 to 6 hours on air, to 40 to 80 minutes on 100% oxygen, to around 20 minutes at 3 ATA. It also displaces CO from cytochrome oxidase and myoglobin.
  • Hyperoxic vasoconstriction with increased oxygen delivery — reduces oedema while still improving tissue oxygenation; useful in crush injury and compartment-threatened tissue.
  • Antibacterial effects — directly toxic to anaerobes (which lack superoxide dismutase), inhibits clostridial alpha-toxin production, and restores oxygen-dependent neutrophil killing.
  • Promotion of angiogenesis, fibroblast proliferation and collagen cross-linking — the rationale in chronic hypoxic wounds and radiation-damaged tissue.

Accepted indications

  • Decompression illness and arterial gas embolism — the unambiguous, time-critical indications.
  • Carbon monoxide poisoning — in practice offered for loss of consciousness, neurological signs, pregnancy, cardiac ischaemia, or a high carboxyhaemoglobin level. The evidence for preventing delayed neurological sequelae is genuinely conflicting, and transferring an unstable patient to a chamber has its own risk — a point worth making in a viva rather than reciting a threshold.
  • Clostridial myonecrosis (gas gangrene) and necrotising fasciitis, as an adjunct to surgery and antibiotics — never instead of them.
  • Crush injury, compartment syndrome and threatened flaps or grafts.
  • Osteoradionecrosis and soft tissue radiation injury; refractory osteomyelitis; selected non-healing diabetic foot ulcers.
  • Severe anaemia where transfusion is impossible; central retinal artery occlusion; intracranial abscess.
  • Thermal burns — used in some centres, with weak evidence.

Decompression illness

TypeFeatures
Type I — "the bends"Musculoskeletal pain (classically periarticular, in large joints), skin itching and marbling (cutis marmorata), and lymphatic swelling. Unpleasant but not immediately dangerous
Type II — seriousNeurological — spinal cord most often, with paraesthesiae, weakness, girdle pain and bladder dysfunction; vestibular ("the staggers"); cardiorespiratory ("the chokes") with chest pain, cough and dyspnoea from pulmonary bubble load
Arterial gas embolismFrom pulmonary barotrauma on ascent — breath-holding while ascending ruptures alveoli and air enters the pulmonary veins. Presents immediately on surfacing with stroke-like symptoms or cardiac arrest. Often grouped with DCI as "decompression illness"

Management, and the two things never to do

  • 100% oxygen by tight-fitting mask, lie flat, fluids, and urgent referral for recompression on a standard table (US Navy Table 6 is the usual starting point for serious DCI). Delay worsens outcome but recompression is still worthwhile even after many hours.
  • Head-down (Trendelenburg) positioning is no longer recommended — it raises intracranial pressure and worsens cerebral oedema without reliably trapping bubbles. Flat is the position.
  • Never allow air transfer at altitude without cabin pressurisation to sea level — the fall in ambient pressure expands every bubble and can convert a type I illness into a catastrophe. Keep the cabin altitude as low as possible, ideally below 300 m.
  • Other diving injuries to distinguish: nitrogen narcosis (reversible on ascent, "the martini effect", from the anaesthetic effect of nitrogen at depth); oxygen toxicity — CNS seizures acutely above about 1.6 to 2 ATA, and pulmonary toxicity with prolonged exposure; barotrauma of ear, sinus and lung; and immersion pulmonary oedema.

Risks of the chamber itself

HazardDetail
BarotraumaMiddle ear is the commonest complication by far — requires active equalisation, decongestants, and sometimes myringotomy or grommets in a ventilated patient who cannot equalise. Also sinus, dental and pulmonary barotrauma
PneumothoraxAn untreated pneumothorax is an absolute contraindication — it will expand on decompression and tension. It must be drained first, with a drain that functions at pressure (a Heimlich valve or an underwater seal that cannot be overwhelmed)
Oxygen toxicityCNS seizures (aura, twitching, then convulsion) and pulmonary toxicity; managed by air breaks within the treatment table
FireThe catastrophic risk. An oxygen-enriched, pressurised environment ignites easily and burns ferociously. No electrical equipment, oils, alcohol-based products, or synthetic clothing; cotton garments, strict no-ignition policy, and deluge systems
ConfinementClaustrophobia and anxiety; in a monoplace chamber the patient is inaccessible, and in a multiplace chamber the attendant is themselves exposed and subject to decompression limits
OtherTransient myopic shift, cataract with prolonged courses, hypoglycaemia in diabetics

Anaesthesia and critical care in a chamber

  • Anything containing air expands and contracts: the tracheal tube cuff must be filled with saline or continuously adjusted, and air splints, drip chambers and the stomach all need attention.
  • Gas-driven and electronic equipment behaves differently. Flowmeters and vaporisers are calibrated at 1 atmosphere: a rotameter under-reads at pressure (the gas is denser, so the bobbin floats at a lower level for the same mass flow), while a plenum vaporiser delivers a lower partial pressure of agent for the same dial setting, since the saturated vapour pressure is unchanged while total pressure has risen. Agent monitoring, not the dial, must guide you.
  • Volatile anaesthesia is generally avoided — fire risk, scavenging difficulty and these calibration problems. Total intravenous anaesthesia with electrically safe, chamber-approved pumps is preferred.
  • Monitoring is limited: electrical equipment must be specifically approved, and in a monoplace chamber you may have little more than a waveform through a penetrator.
  • Nitrous oxide is contraindicated — it dissolves into every gas space and then expands on decompression.
  • Practical reality: the patient must be stable enough for the journey and for a period of inaccessibility. Transferring a patient to a chamber is itself an intervention with risk, and that judgement — rather than the indication list — is usually what is being tested.
