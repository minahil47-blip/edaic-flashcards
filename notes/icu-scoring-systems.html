ICU scoring systems

APACHE, SAPS, SOFA, TISS and the rest — what each one measures, when it is measured, and the single conceptual point that unites them.

The one idea to hold onto

Four different jobs

  • Severity-of-illness / outcome prediction — APACHE, SAPS, MPM. Measured on admission from the worst values in the first 24 hours, and never recalculated.
  • Organ dysfunction — SOFA, MODS, LODS. Measured repeatedly to describe the course of the illness.
  • Nursing and therapeutic workload — TISS, NEMS, NAS. Measured for staffing and costing, not for prognosis.
  • Triage and early warning — NEWS2, qSOFA, MEWS. Bedside prompts to escalate, not diagnostic tests.
  • The critical caveat: all of these are validated for populations, not individuals. A score predicts the mortality of a group of similar patients; it must never be used alone to make a treatment-withdrawal decision for one patient. This is the single most examined point about scoring systems.

The limitations that get asked

  • It needs the admission diagnosis to generate a mortality prediction — the physiology score alone is not enough.
  • It is calculated once, from the first 24 hours, and not repeated. It therefore says nothing about response to treatment.
  • It performs badly after certain admissions — burns, coronary artery bypass grafting, and transfers from another ICU (where the physiology has already been corrected, a problem called lead-time bias).
  • Treatment can improve the score and worsen the prediction's accuracy: a patient resuscitated well before ICU admission scores lower than their true severity.
  • Calibration drifts over time as practice improves, so old equations over-predict mortality in a modern cohort. This is why newer versions and recalibrations keep appearing.

SAPS and MPM

  • SAPS — Simplified Acute Physiology Score. A European development, designed to be simpler than APACHE and, importantly, not to require the admission diagnosis.
  • SAPS II: 17 variables — 12 physiological, plus age, type of admission (medical, scheduled surgical, unscheduled surgical) and three underlying diseases (metastatic cancer, haematological malignancy, AIDS). Gives a predicted mortality directly.
  • SAPS 3 adds information about the patient before ICU admission — comorbidity, location before admission, reason for admission — reflecting the recognition that pre-ICU events matter.
  • MPM — Mortality Probability Model. Uses mostly yes/no variables and gives a probability of death at the moment of admission (MPM₀) rather than after 24 hours, which is its distinctive advantage: it is not contaminated by the first day's treatment.
  • ICNARC model (UK) is the one used for national comparative audit, recalibrated on UK data.

How a model is judged

  • Discrimination — can it separate survivors from non-survivors? Measured by the area under the ROC curve; above 0.8 is good.
  • Calibration — does the predicted mortality match the observed at every level of risk? Tested with the Hosmer–Lemeshow statistic.
  • Standardised mortality ratio = observed deaths ÷ predicted deaths. Below 1 means fewer deaths than predicted. It is used to compare units — but it depends entirely on the model being well calibrated for that case mix, so a low SMR can reflect admission policy rather than better care.

SOFA, qSOFA and sepsis

  • Sepsis-3 defines sepsis as infection plus an acute rise in SOFA of ≥ 2, which corresponds to a mortality of around 10%.
  • qSOFA is the bedside screen, outside ICU, needing no laboratory tests: respiratory rate ≥ 22, altered mentation, systolic BP ≤ 100. Two or more flags risk. It is a prompt, not a diagnosis, and has poorer sensitivity than NEWS2 for detecting deterioration.
  • SIRS criteria are no longer part of the sepsis definition — too non-specific, and absent in a significant minority of patients who went on to die of infection.
  • MODS and LODS are alternative organ-failure scores; SOFA is the one that has stuck because it is simple and is embedded in the sepsis definition.

TISS and workload scores

  • TISS — Therapeutic Intervention Scoring System. It scores the interventions the patient receives, not their physiology. The original had 76 items weighted 1 to 4 points; TISS-28 reduced it to 28 items for practicality.
  • What it is for: measuring nursing workload, staffing requirements and cost, and for classifying the level of care a patient is receiving. Roughly, one nurse per shift can deliver about 40 to 50 TISS points.
  • The crucial distinction: a high TISS means the patient is receiving a lot of treatment, which is not the same as being severely ill. A patient on multiple therapies may be improving; a patient on minimal therapy may be dying. TISS is not a severity score and must not be used to predict outcome.
  • NEMS (Nine Equivalents of Nursing Manpower Score) and NAS (Nursing Activities Score) are the successors, simpler and more nursing-focused.
  • Levels of care (UK): Level 0 ward · Level 1 ward with critical care input · Level 2 single organ support or one nurse to two patients · Level 3 advanced respiratory support or two or more organs supported, one nurse to one patient.

Four traps

  • APACHE / SAPS are calculated once; SOFA is repeated. Mixing these up is the commonest error.
  • TISS measures workload, not severity.
  • APACHE II needs the admission diagnosis; SAPS II does not.
  • No score is valid for an individual decision. They inform, benchmark and audit; they do not decide.
