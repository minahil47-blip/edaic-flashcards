Infusion devices, TCI and cancer pain

How pumps fail, what a TCI model actually does, and the opioid conversions and palliative principles that get examined.

Infusion devices

DeviceMechanismUse and limitation
Syringe driverLead screw advancing the plungerMost accurate at low flow — vasoactive drugs, anaesthetic infusions. Limited by syringe volume
Volumetric pumpLinear or rotary peristaltic, or cassetteLarger volumes, higher rates. Less accurate at very low rates
ElastomericElastic reservoir + flow restrictorPortable, no power. Flow varies with temperature, fill volume, viscosity and restrictor position — simplicity traded for accuracy
PCA pumpBolus on demandMust have a lockout, a maximum dose limit, a secure reservoir and a one-way valve on any co-infused line

The four hazards to be able to explain

  • Siphoning — pump above the patient plus a detached or cracked plunger delivers the whole syringe under gravity. Prevented by an anti-siphon valve and by keeping the pump at patient level.
  • Retrograde flow and bolusing — during occlusion, drug is diverted up a parallel line and then delivered as a bolus when the occlusion clears. Prevented by a one-way (anti-reflux) valve on each co-infused line and minimal dead space.
  • Free flow — a volumetric giving set removed from the pump without an integral anti-free-flow clamp allows unrestricted gravity flow.
  • Delayed occlusion alarm at low rates — the pressure needed to trigger the alarm takes many minutes to build at 2 mL/h, so an occluded noradrenaline infusion can go undetected. Compliant tubing adds a further lag to any rate change.

Target-controlled infusion

A TCI pump runs a population pharmacokinetic model to compute a bolus followed by exponentially decreasing rates. It predicts a concentration; it measures nothing.

ModelCovariatesNote
Marsh (propofol)Weight onlyCannot be adjusted for age; not for children. Effect-site targeting with Marsh gives a large induction bolus
Schnider (propofol)Age, height, weight, lean body massPreferred for effect-site targeting, since its ke0 was derived for that purpose. The LBM equation misbehaves at very high BMI
Eleveld (propofol)Age, weight, sex, and PMAGeneral-purpose: children to the elderly, lean to obese. Increasingly the default
Minto (remifentanil)Age, lean body massRequirements fall substantially with age, so the adjustment matters

The kinetic terms

  • ke0 — the rate constant for transfer between plasma and effect site. A large ke0 means rapid equilibration (remifentanil); a small one means a long delay to peak effect.
  • Effect-site targeting deliberately overshoots the plasma to drive drug into the brain faster: quicker induction, but a greater haemodynamic hit.
  • Context-sensitive half-time — time for the plasma concentration to halve after stopping an infusion, and it depends on how long the infusion ran. Far more useful than elimination half-life for predicting wake-up.
  • Remifentanil's context-sensitive half-time stays at 3 to 5 minutes however long it runs, because of organ-independent ester hydrolysis. That is its defining property — and it means analgesia must be established before it is stopped.
  • Propofol infusion syndrome — above about 4 mg/kg/h beyond 48 h: metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia, lipaemia, cardiac failure.

TIVA safety — the NAP5 lesson

  • A dedicated cannula, or at least a visible infusion site, is mandatory. Disconnection or extravasation is otherwise undetectable, and unrecognised infusion failure has caused awareness.
  • Processed EEG depth monitoring is recommended whenever TIVA is combined with neuromuscular blockade, because the clinical signs of light anaesthesia are abolished and the pump's prediction becomes the only guide.
  • NAP5 found most accidental awareness occurred at induction and emergence — around neuromuscular blockade, transfer and the gap between techniques — not during maintenance. Thiopentone, rapid sequence induction and obstetrics were over-represented.
  • A background infusion removes the intrinsic safety of PCA in an opioid-naive patient: a sedated patient stops pressing, but a background infusion continues. Reserve it for the opioid-tolerant.

Cancer and palliative pain

The WHO ladder — non-opioid, weak opioid, strong opioid, with adjuvants at every step. In cancer pain it is now acceptable to start at the step the pain requires rather than climb from the bottom.

ConversionRatio
Breakthrough doseOne sixth of the 24-hour total — not one tenth
Oral → subcutaneous morphine≈ 2 : 1 (60 mg oral ≈ 30 mg SC/24 h)
Oral → intravenous morphine≈ 3 : 1
OxycodoneAbout twice as potent as oral morphine
Fentanyl patch25 µg/h ≈ 90 mg oral morphine daily — an easy and dangerous place to under-estimate
Opioid rotationStart the new drug 25 to 50% below the calculated equianalgesic dose — cross-tolerance is incomplete

Choosing the opioid

  • Oral morphine remains first-line: modified-release regularly, immediate-release for breakthrough.
  • Severe renal impairment — morphine's active metabolites (morphine-6-glucuronide) accumulate. Use alfentanil, fentanyl, oxycodone or buprenorphine.
  • Transdermal fentanyl is for stable pain only. It takes 12 to 24 h to reach steady state and as long to wear off, so it is never for titration or for an opioid-naive patient in crisis.
  • Methadone has a half-life of 15 to 60 hours with accumulation, plus NMDA antagonism useful in neuropathic and opioid-tolerant pain — specialist supervision only.
  • Tolerance develops to nausea and sedation but never to constipation — prescribe a stimulant laxative with every strong opioid and continue it.
  • Opioid-induced hyperalgesia is made worse by escalation. The response is dose reduction or rotation, with ketamine or an alpha-2 agonist as adjunct.

Adjuvants, interventions and the emergencies

  • Neuropathic pain: amitriptyline, gabapentinoids, duloxetine — alongside the opioid, not instead of it. Dexamethasone where nerve or cord compression contributes.
  • Bone metastases: NSAIDs, bisphosphonates or denosumab, and single-fraction radiotherapy, which relieves pain in most patients.
  • Blocks — the discrimination to memorise: coeliac plexus for upper abdominal viscera (pancreatic cancer), superior hypogastric (presacral) plexus for pelvic viscera, ganglion impar for the perineum and rectum. Coeliac block causes hypotension and diarrhoea from sympathetic blockade.
  • Intrathecal drug delivery for refractory pain — morphine with bupivacaine and clonidine, ziconotide in specialist practice; analgesia at a fraction of the systemic dose.
  • Metastatic spinal cord compression is an emergency: high-dose dexamethasone and whole-spine MRI within 24 hours, then radiotherapy or surgery. Delay costs ambulation permanently.
  • Hypercalcaemia of malignancy: confusion, nausea, constipation, polyuria and thirst — easily mistaken for disease progression or opioid effect. Rehydrate, then a bisphosphonate.

End-of-life principles

  • Total pain (Cicely Saunders) — physical, psychological, social and spiritual. It explains why pain uncontrolled by escalating opioid may need an entirely different intervention.
  • Anticipatory prescribing: an opioid for pain and breathlessness, midazolam for agitation, an antimuscarinic for secretions, and an antiemetic — all as required, subcutaneously.
  • Opioids relieve breathlessness independently of their analgesic action; low-dose morphine reduces the sensation, with a benzodiazepine for associated anxiety.
  • Palliative sedation is not euthanasia. The intent is relief of refractory symptoms, it is titrated to symptom control, and the evidence does not show that it shortens survival. Intent is what distinguishes it ethically.
  • Nausea is treated by mechanism, not interchangeably: haloperidol and levomepromazine, cyclizine, metoclopramide, ondansetron.
