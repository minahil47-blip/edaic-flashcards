Local anaesthetics, LAST, blocks and ultrasound

LA pharmacology and the pKa/protein-binding logic, systemic toxicity and lipid rescue, ultrasound physics, the common blocks, and neuraxial anaesthesia with anticoagulants.

LA pharmacology

Every clinical property follows from three numbers: pKa → speed of onset, lipid solubility → potency, protein binding → duration.

The reasoning you must be able to state

  • Mechanism: the unionised base crosses the membrane, then the ionised form blocks the voltage-gated sodium channel from inside, binding preferentially to the inactivated state. Hence use-dependent block of rapidly firing fibres.
  • Closer pKa to physiological pH → more unionised drug → faster onset. Lidocaine (7.9) is faster than bupivacaine (8.1). Chloroprocaine is the exception — a high pKa yet rapid onset, because it is used at high concentration.
  • Infected or acidic tissue traps the drug in its ionised form and the block fails — the pharmacological reason local anaesthesia does not work well in an abscess.
  • Adding bicarbonate speeds onset by increasing the unionised fraction.
  • Adrenaline 1:200,000 prolongs the block, reduces peak plasma concentration and raises the permissible dose. Avoid in digits and in the presence of fixed cardiac output disease; it is not useful for highly vascular ropivacaine blocks.
  • Block order: B fibres (autonomic) → C and Aδ (pain, temperature) → Aγ, Aβ → Aα (motor) last. So sympathetic block extends highest in a spinal, then sensory, then motor — the classic two dermatome difference.
  • Esters (cocaine, procaine, chloroprocaine, amethocaine — one "i" in the name) are hydrolysed by plasma cholinesterase and produce PABA, hence true allergy. Amides (two "i"s) are hepatically metabolised, and allergy is almost always to the preservative.

Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity

  1. 1

    Recognise

    Classically progressive: circumoral tingling, metallic taste, tinnitus, visual disturbance, slurred speech, agitation → twitching and convulsions → coma and respiratory arrest → cardiovascular collapse. In practice it may present as cardiac arrest or seizure with no prodrome, and may be delayed for up to an hour.

  2. 2

    Stop and support

    Stop injecting, call for help, 100% oxygen, secure the airway, hyperventilate (alkalosis reduces free drug), control seizures with a benzodiazepine — small doses of thiopentone or propofol are acceptable but propofol is not a substitute for lipid emulsion.

  3. 3

    Lipid emulsion

    20% lipid emulsion: bolus 1.5 mL/kg over 1 min, then infusion 15 mL/kg/h. Up to two further boluses 5 min apart and the infusion rate may be doubled; maximum of about 12 mL/kg in total.

  4. 4

    Modified cardiac arrest management

    Standard ALS but prolonged — recovery may take over an hour, so continue CPR and consider cardiopulmonary bypass. Avoid lidocaine as an antiarrhythmic, and use small incremental doses of adrenaline (< 1 µg/kg). Vasopressin, calcium channel blockers and beta blockers are avoided.

Prevention, which is the real answer

  • Calculate the maximum dose for the actual weight before you start, and use the lowest effective concentration and volume.
  • Aspirate before and during injection, inject incrementally, and watch the needle tip under ultrasound. Ultrasound reduces but does not abolish LAST.
  • Highest plasma concentrations come from intercostal and interpleural blocks and from tracheal or mucosal topical application; lowest from subcutaneous infiltration and sciatic/femoral blocks.
  • Lipid emulsion must be immediately available wherever large-dose local anaesthesia is performed, with the LAST cognitive aid displayed beside it.

Ultrasound physics

PrincipleConsequence
Piezoelectric effectA crystal deforms in an electric field and generates charge when deformed — so the same element transmits and receives. Lead zirconate titanate is the usual material
Frequency trade-offHigher frequency = better resolution, less penetration. 10 to 15 MHz for interscalene and axillary; 2 to 5 MHz for a deep sciatic or neuraxial view
Axial vs lateralAxial resolution depends on wavelength (so on frequency); lateral resolution depends on beam width and is best at the focal zone
Acoustic impedanceReflection occurs at interfaces of differing impedance. A large mismatch (soft tissue to air, or to bone) reflects nearly everything — hence coupling gel, and the bright line with shadow behind bone
AttenuationAbsorption, scatter and reflection; roughly proportional to frequency — the physical reason high frequency cannot go deep
DopplerFrequency shift from moving blood. Needs the beam parallel to flow; at 90° the measured velocity is zero

Artefacts you must name

  • Anisotropy — a structure's echogenicity changes with beam angle. Tendons and nerves can vanish when the probe tilts; the commonest reason a nerve is "not there".
  • Acoustic shadowing behind bone or calcification; acoustic enhancement deep to a fluid-filled structure.
  • Reverberation and comet tail from strong parallel reflectors, including needles.
  • Mirror image and side-lobe artefacts place structures where they are not.
  • Nerves look different by site: the brachial plexus roots are hypoechoic, while peripheral nerves are typically honeycomb — hypoechoic fascicles in a hyperechoic connective tissue matrix.

Nerve injury and needling

  • Intraneural injection and high injection pressure cause injury. Resistance to injection, pain on injection, or motor response below 0.2 mA all mean the needle is too close — stop and withdraw.
  • Permanent block-related neurological injury is rare (of the order of 2 to 4 per 10,000); transient symptoms are far commoner, and most resolve within weeks.
  • Most perioperative nerve injury is not caused by the block — positioning, tourniquet, surgical traction and pre-existing neuropathy account for much of it, which is why a pre-block neurological record matters.
  • Stop Before You Block — the final check of the side immediately before needle insertion.

Vertebral canal haematoma

  • Catheter removal carries the same risk as insertion and must obey the same intervals — the most commonly forgotten point.
  • Suspect it with escalating back pain, unexpectedly dense or progressing motor block, or block that does not regress as expected. Bladder and bowel dysfunction are late.
  • Urgent MRI and decompression, ideally within 8 hours of symptom onset — outcome depends on the speed of surgery, so the chain from nurse to neurosurgeon must be rehearsed.
  • Regular, documented motor block scoring is what makes early detection possible; a low-concentration epidural infusion that permits movement is part of the safety design.
  • Contraindications to neuraxial block: patient refusal, local or systemic sepsis, raised intracranial pressure, uncorrected coagulopathy or thrombocytopenia, and hypovolaemia. Fixed cardiac output states (severe aortic stenosis) are a major relative contraindication.
