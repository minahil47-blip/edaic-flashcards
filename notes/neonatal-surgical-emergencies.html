Neonatal surgical emergencies

Tracheo-oesophageal fistula, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, abdominal wall defects, necrotising enterocolitis and pyloric stenosis — and the neonatal physiology that makes them dangerous.

Numbers to have ready

  • Normal values at term: heart rate 120 to 160, respiratory rate 30 to 60, systolic BP 60 to 80 mmHg, circulating volume 80 to 90 mL/kg.
  • Tracheal tube: uncuffed or microcuffed 3.0 to 3.5 mm at term, length about 9 to 10 cm at the lips. The narrowest point is the cricoid in the infant, and the larynx is higher (C3–C4) with a floppy, omega-shaped epiglottis — hence a straight blade.
  • Maintenance fluid — the 4/2/1 rule: 4 mL/kg/h for the first 10 kg, 2 for the next 10, 1 thereafter. Neonates need glucose-containing maintenance with isotonic sodium; hypotonic fluids cause hyponatraemic encephalopathy.
  • Retinopathy of prematurity — avoid hyperoxia in the preterm infant; target saturations are deliberately lower (commonly 90 to 95%).

Tracheo-oesophageal fistula & oesophageal atresia

  • Incidence about 1 in 3,000. The commonest type by far — around 85% — is oesophageal atresia with a DISTAL tracheo-oesophageal fistula (Gross type C): the upper oesophagus ends blindly and the lower segment connects to the trachea.
  • Presentation: polyhydramnios antenatally; then drooling, choking and cyanosis on feeding, and a nasogastric tube that coils in the upper pouch on radiograph. Gas in the stomach confirms a distal fistula.
  • VACTERL association — Vertebral, Anorectal, Cardiac, Tracheo-Esophageal, Renal, Limb. So echocardiography and renal ultrasound before surgery: roughly half have another anomaly, and a duct-dependent cardiac lesion changes everything.
  • Preoperative care: head up, continuous suction of the upper pouch, no oral feeding, antibiotics for aspiration pneumonitis.

The anaesthetic problem, and why it is examined

  • Positive pressure ventilation can pass preferentially down the fistula into the stomach, causing gastric distension that splints the diaphragm and makes ventilation progressively impossible. In the worst case the stomach perforates.
  • So the aim is to site the tube tip between the fistula and the carina, with the bevel directed anteriorly, confirmed by auscultation or bronchoscopy.
  • Many centres intubate with the infant breathing spontaneously, or ventilate gently with low pressures, avoiding vigorous mask ventilation before the airway is secured.
  • A pre-existing gastrostomy can vent the stomach but, if the fistula is large, gas escapes through it and ventilation fails — a classic examination scenario.
  • Intraoperatively the surgeon's retraction of the right lung and compression of the trachea cause desaturation and tube obstruction — frequent suction and good communication are the whole job. Thoracoscopic repair adds CO₂ insufflation and hypercapnia.
  • Postoperative: anastomotic leak and stricture, recurrent fistula, tracheomalacia, and gastro-oesophageal reflux which is almost universal.

Congenital diaphragmatic hernia

  • About 1 in 3,000; 85 to 90% are left-sided posterolateral (Bochdalek). Anterior Morgagni hernias are rarer and present later and more benignly.
  • The lethal problem is not the hernia, it is the lung: pulmonary hypoplasia (bilateral, worse ipsilaterally) with abnormal, hyper-reactive pulmonary vasculature causing pulmonary hypertension. Outcome is determined by lung development, not by the size of the defect.
  • Presentation: respiratory distress at birth, scaphoid abdomen, absent breath sounds with bowel sounds in the chest, mediastinal shift, apparent "dextrocardia" on the left-sided variety.
  • Immediate management is the examinable part: intubate and ventilate — do NOT bag-mask ventilate, as that inflates the herniated bowel and worsens the lung compression. Pass a nasogastric tube to decompress, and never attempt to insert a chest drain into what looks like a pneumothorax.
  • "Gentle ventilation" is the modern strategy: low peak pressures (aiming below about 25 cmH₂O), permissive hypercapnia, accepting preductal saturations of 85 to 95%, and delayed surgical repair after physiological stabilisation — a change from the old emergency-surgery approach, and one that improved survival.
  • Pulmonary hypertension management: avoid hypoxia, acidosis and cold; inhaled nitric oxide, sildenafil, and ECMO as a bridge in selected cases. High-frequency oscillation is used in some centres.

Anaesthetic issues common to both

  • Fluid, heat and glucose — the three things to get right before anything else. Warm the room to 26 °C or more.
  • Nasogastric decompression, a rapid sequence or modified induction for a full, obstructed abdomen, and avoidance of nitrous oxide, which distends bowel and makes closure impossible.
  • Primary closure may cause abdominal compartment syndrome: watch for a rise in airway pressure, a fall in compliance, oliguria, lower limb congestion and acidosis. A staged closure with a silo is used when primary closure would be too tight — the surgeon and anaesthetist decide this together, from the ventilation and perfusion, not from appearance alone.
  • Plan for postoperative ventilation, prolonged ileus and parenteral nutrition.

Necrotising enterocolitis & pyloric stenosis

ConditionWhat to know
Necrotising enterocolitisPreterm, formula-fed infant in the first weeks. Distension, bloody stools, bilious aspirates, and pneumatosis intestinalis on radiograph; portal venous gas and free gas indicate severe disease. Bell staging. Management is medical first — stop feeds, decompress, antibiotics, parenteral nutrition — with surgery for perforation or deterioration. These are the sickest neonates an anaesthetist meets: septic, coagulopathic, thrombocytopenic, acidotic, often ventilated already, and best managed with a high-dose opioid technique, blood products ready, and no expectation of extubation
Pyloric stenosisTypically a firstborn male at 3 to 6 weeks, with projectile non-bilious vomiting, visible peristalsis and a palpable olive. It is a medical emergency, not a surgical one — the operation waits for the biochemistry

Pyloric stenosis biochemistry — a guaranteed question

  • Loss of gastric contents (H⁺, Cl⁻, K⁺, Na⁺, water) produces a hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic ALKALOSIS.
  • The paradox: the kidney conserves sodium and water at the expense of hydrogen ions, so the urine becomes paradoxically acidic despite a systemic alkalosis.
  • Resuscitate before theatre: 0.9% saline with added potassium, continued until chloride > 100 mmol/L, bicarbonate < 26 to 30 mmol/L and sodium > 135 mmol/L with normal potassium. Correcting the chloride is the key to correcting the alkalosis.
  • Why it matters anaesthetically: an alkalotic infant has a blunted respiratory drive and a left-shifted CSF pH, and is at real risk of postoperative apnoea — which is the whole reason for delaying surgery.
  • Technique: empty the stomach with a wide-bore tube in several positions, then rapid sequence induction. Short-acting agents, local infiltration or a rectus sheath block, paracetamol, and avoid long-acting opioids. Extubate awake, and monitor for apnoea afterwards.
