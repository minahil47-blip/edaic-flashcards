Neuroanaesthesia: ICP, CPP, neuroprotection and the sitting position

Cerebral physiology as it is actually examined, the management of raised intracranial pressure, venous air embolism, and brain stem death testing.

Monro–Kellie and why it matters

  • The cranium is a fixed box containing brain (≈ 80%), blood (≈ 10%) and CSF (≈ 10%). An increase in one must be matched by a decrease in another or ICP rises.
  • Compensation is initially effective, then abruptly fails — the intracranial compliance curve is flat then steep. This is why a patient can look stable and then deteriorate within minutes.
  • CSF: about 150 mL total, produced at 0.35 mL/min (≈ 500 mL/day) by the choroid plexus, so it turns over three to four times a day. Absorbed at the arachnoid villi.
  • Cushing's reflex — hypertension, bradycardia and irregular respiration — is a late sign of critically raised ICP, not an early warning.

Reducing ICP — a tiered approach

  1. 1

    Position and venous drainage

    30° head up, neck neutral, no tight collar or tapes, no ties round the neck. Free and costless. Avoid coughing, straining and PEEP beyond what oxygenation requires.

  2. 2

    Physiology

    Normocapnia 4.5 to 5.0 kPa, normoxia, normoglycaemia, normothermia (treat pyrexia aggressively), adequate sedation and analgesia, and MAP sufficient for CPP 60 to 70.

  3. 3

    Osmotherapy

    Mannitol 0.25 to 1 g/kg — rapid, but causes diuresis, hypovolaemia, hypernatraemia then rebound, and needs an intact blood–brain barrier. Hypertonic saline is an alternative that expands intravascular volume rather than depleting it, with a serum sodium target typically 145 to 155 mmol/L.

  4. 4

    CSF drainage and surgery

    External ventricular drain, evacuation of a mass lesion, or decompressive craniectomy — which reliably lowers ICP but, in DECRA and RESCUEicp, traded mortality for survival with greater disability. The decision is not purely physiological.

  5. 5

    Last resorts

    Thiopentone coma titrated to burst suppression (hypotension, immunosuppression, long context-sensitive half-time); short-term hyperventilation to 3.5 to 4 kPa only for impending herniation; therapeutic hypothermia, which has repeatedly failed to show benefit in trials (EUROTHERM).

Drugs and the brain

  • All intravenous agents except ketamine reduce CMRO₂ and CBF with preserved coupling — propofol and thiopentone are the agents of choice for a tight brain.
  • Ketamine's reputation is outdated: in the ventilated, sedated patient it does not raise ICP, and it supports blood pressure. In the spontaneously breathing patient with a mass lesion, caution remains.
  • Volatiles uncouple flow and metabolism — they reduce CMRO₂ but vasodilate, so CBF and ICP rise. The effect is dose-dependent and minimal below about 1 MAC; isoflurane and sevoflurane are acceptable at low dose, and all volatiles also blunt autoregulation and CO₂ reactivity.
  • Nitrous oxide raises CMRO₂, CBF and ICP and expands intracranial air — avoid in neurosurgery, particularly after a posterior fossa or sitting-position case.
  • Suxamethonium causes a small, transient ICP rise, clinically unimportant beside the consequences of a failed airway — do not withhold it when it is indicated.
  • Opioids are essentially neutral provided ventilation is controlled; hypercapnia from spontaneous breathing is what raises ICP.

The sitting position & venous air embolism

AspectDetail
Why sitPosterior fossa and cervical access with gravity drainage of blood and CSF, a bloodless field, and better access to the midline
The costsHypotension and reduced cardiac output, cerebral hypoperfusion (the arterial line must be zeroed at the external auditory meatus, not the heart — about 20 mmHg lower than at the right atrium), pneumocephalus, macroglossia, quadriplegia from extreme neck flexion, nerve and pressure injuries
Air embolism riskAny open, non-collapsible vein above the level of the heart — dural venous sinuses, emissary and diploic veins in bone. Reported in a high proportion of sitting cases when sought with TOE
Most sensitive monitorTransoesophageal echocardiography, then precordial Doppler. Both detect air long before a fall in EtCO₂ or SpO₂
Clinical signsSudden fall in EtCO₂, rising EtN₂, hypotension, arrhythmia, desaturation, and a "mill-wheel" murmur (a late and uncommon sign)

Managing venous air embolism

  • Tell the surgeon and flood the field with saline or wet packs; the surgeon identifies and occludes the entry point, and bone wax seals diploic veins.
  • Stop nitrous oxide, give 100% oxygen — nitrous expands the bubble.
  • Lower the head and apply bilateral jugular compression if feasible to raise venous pressure and stop further entrainment.
  • Durant's position — left lateral and head down — moves the air bubble away from the right ventricular outflow tract. Aspirate from a right atrial catheter.
  • Support the circulation with fluid and vasopressors, and consider hyperbaric oxygen if paradoxical embolism has caused cerebral injury.
  • A patent foramen ovale (present in about 25 to 30% of adults) permits paradoxical embolism and is a relative contraindication to the sitting position — some centres screen for it.

Specific procedures

  • Craniotomy for tumour — smooth induction without hypertension, controlled ventilation to normocapnia, modest head-up position, avoid nitrous oxide, dexamethasone for oedema, phenytoin or levetiracetam if indicated, and a smooth emergence so the patient can be neurologically assessed without coughing or hypertension. Remifentanil-based technique suits this well.
  • Awake craniotomy — for lesions near eloquent cortex. Scalp block of the six nerves (supraorbital, supratrochlear, zygomaticotemporal, auriculotemporal, greater and lesser occipital), dexmedetomidine or low-dose propofol/remifentanil, with an asleep–awake–asleep or monitored-anaesthesia-care approach. Key risks: seizure, agitation, airway loss, nausea and pain from dura or vessels.
  • Transsphenoidal pituitary surgery — shared airway, throat pack, risk of diabetes insipidus postoperatively (dilute polyuria with rising sodium), steroid cover, and the specific hazards of acromegaly (difficult airway) and Cushing's.
  • Subarachnoid haemorrhage — coiling or clipping, nimodipine for delayed cerebral ischaemia, euvolaemia (not "triple-H" therapy, now abandoned), and the characteristic complications: hyponatraemia (distinguish SIADH from cerebral salt wasting by volume state), neurogenic pulmonary oedema, ECG changes and stunned myocardium.
  • Spinal surgery — prone positioning risks (eyes, abdominal compression, venous bleeding, airway oedema), postoperative visual loss from ischaemic optic neuropathy, major haemorrhage, and motor-evoked potential monitoring, which precludes neuromuscular blockade and limits volatile dose.
  • Traumatic brain injury — intubate if GCS ≤ 8; a single episode of hypotension or hypoxia worsens outcome, so permissive hypotension is contraindicated. No hypotonic fluids, no glucose-containing maintenance fluid, and no steroids (CRASH showed increased mortality).

Brain stem death

  1. 1

    Preconditions

    A known, irreversible cause of catastrophic brain damage, apnoeic coma on a ventilator. Nothing else may explain the state.

  2. 2

    Exclusions

    No sedatives, opioids or neuromuscular blockers (confirm with a nerve stimulator); no significant metabolic or endocrine derangement; temperature > 34 °C; no profound electrolyte, glucose or acid–base abnormality; no circulatory cause.

  3. 3

    The five brain stem reflexes

    Pupils fixed to light (II–III) · corneal reflex absent (V–VII) · vestibulo-ocular (caloric) absent (VIII–III/VI) · no motor response within the cranial nerve distribution to supraorbital pressure (V–VII) · no gag or cough to pharyngeal and tracheal stimulation (IX–X).

  4. 4

    Apnoea test

    Performed last. Preoxygenate, allow PaCO₂ to rise above 6.0 kPa with pH below 7.40, disconnect with oxygen insufflation and observe for no respiratory effort over 5 minutes while PaCO₂ rises by at least 0.5 kPa.

  5. 5

    Who, and when

    Two doctors, both registered more than 5 years, at least one a consultant, neither a member of the transplant team. Tested twice. The legal time of death is the time of the first set of tests, although death is confirmed after the second.

Points that are routinely misremembered

  • Spinal reflexes may persist and do not invalidate the diagnosis — including the dramatic "Lazarus sign". Only reflexes mediated by the brain stem are tested.
  • Ancillary tests (EEG, angiography, evoked potentials) are not required in the UK; the diagnosis is clinical. They are used where the clinical examination cannot be completed.
  • Diabetes insipidus is common after brain stem death, with massive dilute polyuria, hypernatraemia and hypovolaemia — treat with desmopressin and fluid.
  • Physiology after brain stem death: loss of vasomotor tone, loss of temperature regulation, a catecholamine surge followed by collapse, and progressive endocrine failure. Donor optimisation targets normovolaemia, noradrenaline or vasopressin, hormone replacement, and lung-protective ventilation.
