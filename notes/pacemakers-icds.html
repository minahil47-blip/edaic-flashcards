Pacemakers & implantable defibrillators for surgery

The NBG code, what diathermy actually does to a device, and the decisions to make before, during and after the operation.

The NBG / NASPE-BPEG code

Five positions. You are expected to read a code and say what the device will do.

PositionMeaningLetters
IChamber pacedO none · A atrium · V ventricle · D dual
IIChamber sensedO · A · V · D
IIIResponse to sensingO none · I inhibited · T triggered · D both
IVRate modulationO none · R rate-responsive
VMultisite pacingO · A · V · D

Read these four without hesitating

  • VVI — paces the ventricle, senses the ventricle, inhibited by a sensed beat. Single-chamber backup; no AV synchrony.
  • DDD — paces and senses both chambers with both responses. Maintains AV synchrony, the physiological mode.
  • DDDR — as above, with rate response to exercise, usually from an accelerometer or minute ventilation.
  • VOO / DOO — asynchronous, no sensing at all. This is what a magnet produces, and what you want when diathermy would otherwise inhibit the device.
  • AAI is only appropriate with intact AV conduction — it offers no ventricular backup if the patient blocks.

Why theatre is hostile to a device

  • Electromagnetic interference, chiefly from monopolar diathermy, is the central problem. The device cannot distinguish diathermy current from intrinsic cardiac activity.
  • In a pacemaker, interference is sensed as cardiac activity and inhibits output — so a dependent patient has no pacing and no output. Interference can also cause inappropriate rate increase, reversion to a noise mode, or, rarely, reprogramming.
  • In an ICD, interference is sensed as ventricular fibrillation and triggers an inappropriate shock — dangerous for the patient and for anyone touching them.
  • Other sources: nerve stimulators, evoked potential monitoring, electroconvulsive therapy, lithotripsy, radiofrequency ablation, MRI, radiotherapy, and surgical robots.
  • Suxamethonium fasciculation can cause myopotential oversensing and inhibition. Shivering does the same.

Diathermy rules

  • Use bipolar diathermy wherever possible — the current path is confined between the forceps tips, so interference is minimal.
  • If monopolar is unavoidable: short bursts at the lowest effective power, with the return plate placed so the current path does not cross the device or its leads — generally on the thigh or buttock for upper-body surgery.
  • Keep diathermy at least 15 cm from the device. Surgery on the chest wall near the generator is the highest-risk situation.

The preoperative decisions

  1. 1

    Identify the device and the indication

    Pacemaker or ICD? Device card, chest radiograph, the latest technician check (ideally within 12 months for a pacemaker, 6 for an ICD). Why was it implanted? Complete heart block is a very different problem from intermittent syncope.

  2. 2

    Is the patient pacing-dependent?

    The single most important question. Dependent means there is no adequate underlying rhythm — inhibition causes asystole. Look at the ECG: if every complex is paced, assume dependence until the technician says otherwise.

  3. 3

    Decide: reprogramme or magnet?

    Pacing-dependent patient having surgery above the umbilicus → reprogramme to an asynchronous mode (DOO or VOO), or apply a magnet. Rate response must be turned off — it can be driven by diathermy, ventilation or surgical movement. For any ICD, the tachyarrhythmia therapy must be disabled (by programming, or by a magnet).

  4. 4

    Have the backup ready

    External pacing and defibrillation pads applied before draping, in a position that does not overlie the generator, with atropine, isoprenaline or adrenaline immediately available. A technician contactable, and the device re-checked and reprogrammed before discharge.

What a magnet actually does — and the trap

  • On a pacemaker, a magnet usually switches it to asynchronous pacing at a fixed rate (often around 85 to 100/min, manufacturer-dependent). That protects against inhibition.
  • On an ICD, a magnet suspends tachyarrhythmia detection and shock therapy but does NOT make the pacing function asynchronous. So a pacing-dependent patient with an ICD is still vulnerable to inhibition with a magnet on — that requires reprogramming.
  • Magnet response is not universal; some devices are programmed to ignore it, and the response can be switched off. Never assume — check the device or ask the technician.
  • A magnet is the emergency measure of choice if an ICD delivers an inappropriate shock intraoperatively: apply it immediately and stop the diathermy.

Pacing modes and haemodynamics

  • Loss of AV synchrony costs roughly 15 to 30% of cardiac output — more in a stiff, hypertrophied or diastolically impaired ventricle. So VOO is a compromise, not a neutral choice.
  • Pacemaker syndrome — fatigue, dyspnoea and hypotension from ventricular pacing with retrograde atrial conduction and loss of synchrony.
  • Temporary transvenous pacing thresholds: capture is confirmed by a paced QRS with a pulse; set output at about twice threshold to allow for displacement, and beware the rise in threshold with hypoxia, acidosis, hyperkalaemia and antiarrhythmics.
  • Transcutaneous pacing is painful and needs sedation; confirm mechanical capture by a pulse or waveform, not by the monitor artefact.
