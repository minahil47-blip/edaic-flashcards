Placental transfer and drugs in pregnancy and breastfeeding

What crosses and what does not, and why — plus ion trapping, the drugs to avoid in each trimester, and the simple rules for lactation.

The placenta as a membrane

At term the placenta offers a large surface area (about 11 to 16 m²) and a thin membrane, with maternal blood flow of roughly 600 to 700 mL/min — around 10% of cardiac output. It is an efficient exchange organ, so the default assumption is that a drug will cross.

Fick's law applied to the placenta

  • Rate of transfer ∝ (surface area × diffusion constant × concentration gradient) ÷ membrane thickness.
  • The diffusion constant depends on the drug: low molecular weight (< 500 Da), high lipid solubility, low protein binding and a low degree of ionisation all favour transfer.
  • So drugs cross poorly if they are large, ionised, highly protein-bound or water-soluble.
  • Placental blood flow is the other determinant — and it is reduced by hypotension, aortocaval compression, uterine contraction, catecholamines and pre-eclampsia, all of which also reduce fetal oxygen delivery.

Ion trapping — the mechanism to be able to explain

  • The fetus is relatively acidotic compared with the mother (fetal pH roughly 0.1 unit lower, and much lower if distressed).
  • A weak base such as a local anaesthetic crosses the placenta in its unionised form, then encounters the more acidic fetal blood and becomes ionised — and the ionised form cannot cross back.
  • So the drug accumulates in the fetus. This is ion trapping, and it is worst with the most ionisable agents and in the most acidotic fetus — exactly the situation in fetal distress.
  • Bupivacaine is less affected than lidocaine because of its very high protein binding (about 95%), so less free drug is available to cross; prilocaine is avoided in obstetrics because of methaemoglobinaemia.
  • The practical point: a distressed, acidotic fetus is the one most vulnerable to trapping local anaesthetic, and it is also the one in which inadvertent intravascular injection is least well tolerated.

Maternal pharmacokinetic changes

  • Increased volume of distribution — plasma volume up about 50%, total body water up — so loading doses of water-soluble drugs may need to be larger.
  • Reduced plasma albumin (dilutional) means a higher free fraction of highly protein-bound drugs, and total measured levels become misleading.
  • Increased minute ventilation and reduced FRC speed inhalational induction, and MAC is reduced by about 25 to 40% — so volatile requirement falls.
  • Reduced local anaesthetic requirement for neuraxial block — by around a third — because of engorged epidural veins reducing the volume of the space, raised intra-abdominal pressure, and increased nerve sensitivity from progesterone.
  • Plasma cholinesterase activity falls by about 25%, but suxamethonium duration is not clinically prolonged because the volume of distribution rises in parallel.
  • Increased renal blood flow and GFR (up roughly 50%) means faster clearance of renally excreted drugs — relevant to antibiotic and LMWH dosing.
  • Delayed gastric emptying in labour (and with opioids), reduced lower oesophageal sphincter tone, and raised intragastric pressure — the basis for aspiration prophylaxis and rapid sequence induction.

Aortocaval compression

  • From about 20 weeks, the supine uterus compresses the inferior vena cava and aorta. Up to 15% of women develop supine hypotensive syndrome, but uteroplacental flow falls before maternal pressure does, so a normal blood pressure is no reassurance.
  • Left lateral tilt of 15°, or full left lateral, or manual uterine displacement. In maternal cardiac arrest, manual left uterine displacement is preferred to tilt because tilt degrades the quality of chest compressions, and perimortem caesarean section is begun within 4 minutes if there is no return of circulation — for the mother's benefit, by relieving the obstruction.

Teratogenicity and the drugs to avoid

PeriodRisk
0 to 2 weeksAll-or-nothing: the conceptus either dies or recovers fully
3 to 8 weeks (organogenesis)The period of maximum teratogenic risk — structural malformation
After 8 weeksGrowth restriction and functional defects rather than gross malformation
Third trimester / labourDirect fetal and neonatal drug effects — respiratory depression, bleeding, ductal closure, withdrawal

The named teratogens

  • Warfarin — nasal and midfacial hypoplasia, stippled epiphyses, CNS anomalies in the first trimester; fetal and placental haemorrhage later. LMWH instead.
  • ACE inhibitors and ARBs — oligohydramnios, renal dysgenesis, skull hypoplasia, especially in the second and third trimesters.
  • Sodium valproate — neural tube defects and a marked reduction in IQ; the highest-risk antiepileptic, and essentially contraindicated in women of childbearing potential without stringent safeguards. Phenytoin and carbamazepine carry lower but real risks; lamotrigine and levetiracetam are preferred. Folic acid for all.
  • Tetracyclines — tooth and bone staining. Aminoglycosides — ototoxicity. Trimethoprim — folate antagonism in the first trimester. Sulfonamides — kernicterus near term. Nitrofurantoin — haemolysis at term.
  • NSAIDs — premature closure of the ductus arteriosus, oligohydramnios and renal impairment in the third trimester; avoid after about 30 weeks.
  • Retinoids, methotrexate, thalidomide, lithium (Ebstein's anomaly), androgens, diethylstilboestrol.
  • DOACs are contraindicated in pregnancy and breastfeeding.
  • Nitrous oxide — a theoretical concern through methionine synthase inhibition; in practice regarded as acceptable in labour and for brief anaesthesia, though many avoid prolonged use in the first trimester.
  • Anaesthesia itself has not been shown to be teratogenic in humans at clinical doses. The risks of non-obstetric surgery in pregnancy come from the underlying illness, hypoxia, hypotension, hypocapnia, hypoglycaemia and preterm labour — so the anaesthetic priorities are maintaining normal maternal physiology, deferring elective surgery (ideally to the second trimester if it must be done), regional technique where feasible, and fetal monitoring with obstetric involvement.

Drugs and breastfeeding

  • Breast milk is slightly acidic relative to plasma, so weak bases accumulate in it by the same ion-trapping logic as the fetus. Lipid-soluble, unionised, low-protein-bound drugs transfer most.
  • In practice almost all anaesthetic drugs are compatible with breastfeeding, and a mother can feed as soon as she is awake and alert enough to hold her baby. "Pump and dump" is almost never required — the quantity in milk is tiny compared with the dose that crossed the placenta before delivery.
  • Avoid: codeine (CYP2D6 ultra-rapid metabolisers have caused neonatal deaths — an absolute avoid), pethidine (norpethidine accumulates), aspirin at analgesic doses (Reye's syndrome), long-acting benzodiazepines, and amiodarone (iodine load).
  • Safe and preferred: paracetamol, ibuprofen, morphine and oxycodone in moderate doses with monitoring of the infant, local anaesthetics, most antibiotics (penicillins, cephalosporins, macrolides), heparin and LMWH, and warfarin (it is highly protein-bound and does not pass into milk in significant amounts — a useful contrast with pregnancy).
  • Radiology: iodinated and gadolinium contrast agents do not require interruption of feeding; radioiodine does.

The three rules that answer most questions

  • Small, lipid-soluble, unionised and unbound → crosses. Large, ionised, polar or highly bound → does not.
  • Quaternary ammonium compounds do not cross — relaxants, glycopyrronium. Tertiary amines do — atropine, hyoscine.
  • Weak bases get trapped on the acidic side — in the fetus, and in milk.
