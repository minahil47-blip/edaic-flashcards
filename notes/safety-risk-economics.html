Human factors, patient safety, occupational risk, economics and VTE

The whole of ETR domain 1.8 and 1.10 — the non-clinical material that is examined far more than trainees expect — plus thromboprophylaxis.

ANTS & human factors

ANTS has four categories, each with observable elements rated 1 (poor) to 4 (good). It assesses only non-technical skills; technical skill is assessed separately.

Error theory you must be able to name

  • Reason's person vs system approach. The person approach blames and retrains the individual; the system approach accepts that competent people err and builds defences around predictable fallibility. The system approach is the correct answer.
  • Swiss cheese model — latent organisational conditions align with active failures; incidents usually require several small failures, not one large one.
  • Slips and lapses are failures of execution in automatic, skill-based performance. Mistakes are failures of planning, in rule-based or knowledge-based reasoning. Violations are deliberate deviations — and a routine violation that has become normal practice is normalisation of deviance.
  • Endsley's three levels of situation awareness: gather, understand, anticipate. Most anaesthetic incidents fail at level 2 or 3, not at data collection.
  • Fixation error is PERSISTING with a plan despite evidence against it — "this and only this", "everything but this", "everything is fine". Not abandoning a diagnosis too quickly.
  • Recognition-primed decision making is what experts actually do under pressure — pattern-match and run the first workable option. Formal analytical weighing is reserved for novel problems with time available.

The behaviours that prevent harm

  • Closed-loop communication — name the person, state the request, hear it repeated back, hear it confirmed done. "Someone give some adrenaline" is the failure it prevents.
  • Graded assertiveness is a way of escalating a concern, not of deferring: PACE (probe, alert, challenge, emergency) and the two-challenge rule, where an unanswered concern raised twice obliges you to act or escalate.
  • A steep authority gradient is one of the best-documented contributors to adverse events. Flattening it by introductions, briefing and explicit invitation to speak up is a safety intervention.
  • Cognitive aids and emergency manuals improve performance, repeatedly demonstrated for MH, LAST and cardiac arrest. A nominated reader who is not the team leader gets the most from them. Recall under stress is unreliable even in experts.
  • Sterile cockpit — minimise non-essential conversation at induction, emergence, drug preparation and handover. Interruption during drug preparation is a documented route to medication error.
  • Leadership is "hands off, eyes on"; role allocation, verbalised plans and periodic summaries maintain shared situation awareness. Stress narrows attention and reduces working memory precisely when analysis is needed.
  • SBAR for handover and escalation; fatigue after extended wakefulness impairs performance comparably to significant alcohol levels, with a real risk driving home.

The framework vocabulary

  • "To Err Is Human" (IOM, 1999) and "An Organisation with a Memory" put medical error on the policy agenda.
  • Never events are serious, largely preventable incidents with strong existing safeguards — wrong-site surgery, retained foreign object, wrong-route administration, misplaced nasogastric tube, wrong implant. They are so called because they should never happen, not because they never do.
  • Hierarchy of controls: design and forcing functions are strongest; education, warnings and written policy are weakest, because they depend entirely on human compliance. Forcing functions in anaesthesia: pin index, non-interchangeable gas connectors, NRFit neuraxial connectors, the oxygen–nitrous interlock.
  • A just culture distinguishes human error and at-risk behaviour, which need a system and coaching response, from recklessness, which does warrant accountability. It is a balance, not an amnesty.
  • Root cause analysis rarely finds a single root cause — the term is itself criticised, and newer frameworks speak of systems-based learning responses.
  • Duty of candour — statutory and professional: notify, apologise, explain, and say what will be done. Apology is explicitly not an admission of liability.
  • Safety-II studies how work succeeds under varying conditions and the gap between work-as-imagined and work-as-done; Safety-I counts failures. SEIPS models the work system as person, tasks, tools, environment and organisation.
  • Donabedian: structure, process, outcome. Audit measures against a standard; quality improvement changes the process through PDSA cycles, driver diagrams and run charts — they are not the same activity.
  • NatSSIPs / LocSSIPs and Stop Before You Block; NCEPOD and NELA for emergency surgical care. The second victim is the clinician harmed psychologically by involvement in an incident.
  • Analyse incidents by asking why the action made sense to them at the time, avoiding hindsight and outcome bias. A checklist completed as a ritual does not work — the behaviour around it carries the benefit.

Anaesthetic gases

  • Active scavenging has four components: collecting, receiving, transfer and disposal — with a reservoir and pressure relief at the receiving system to protect the patient from transmitted pressure. Theatres need at least 15 air changes per hour.
  • The early reports of miscarriage and congenital abnormality came from UNSCAVENGED theatres. Modern studies with effective scavenging show no convincing association — but scavenging must still be used and checked.
  • Nitrous oxide inhibits methionine synthase by oxidising cobalamin: megaloblastic change, and subacute combined degeneration after heavy exposure. It is also a greenhouse gas with a 114-year atmospheric lifetime and is ozone-depleting, which is why pipelines are being decommissioned.
  • Desflurane's global warming potential runs into the thousands against roughly 130 for sevoflurane, and it is used at higher flows. Avoiding desflurane is the single highest-impact environmental choice in anaesthesia, with low fresh gas flow, TIVA and regional techniques next.

Needlestick — the numbers, precisely

  • Hepatitis B up to 30% in a non-immune recipient · hepatitis C about 3% · HIV about 0.3%. Confusing the last two is a classic error.
  • Risk rises with a hollow-bore needle, visible blood, deep injury, a device from an artery or vein, and a high source viral load.
  • Immediately: encourage bleeding, wash with soap and water, do not scrub or suck, report for risk assessment.
  • HIV PEP as soon as possible, ideally within 1 to 2 hours and generally not beyond 72 hours, for 28 days. Hepatitis B: immunoglobulin plus accelerated vaccine for the non-immune. Hepatitis C has no vaccine and no PEP — early PCR detection and direct-acting antivirals, which cure the great majority.

The other occupational hazards

  • Radiation obeys the INVERSE SQUARE law — doubling the distance quarters the dose, so stepping back two metres or behind a screen is the most effective measure. ALARP, lead aprons, thyroid shields, dosimetry, controlled areas. A lead apron protects nothing above the shoulders: the lens is radiosensitive and needs leaded glasses and a ceiling-suspended screen.
  • Laser: wavelength-specific eyewear, controlled access, signage, matt instruments, a laser protection supervisor, and airway precautions (low FiO₂, no nitrous oxide, saline-filled cuff).
  • Surgical smoke contains viable cells, viral DNA and chemical carcinogens — local exhaust ventilation, not a surgical mask alone.
  • Latex causes both type I IgE anaphylaxis and type IV contact dermatitis; powdered gloves aerosolised the allergen and have been withdrawn. Chlorhexidine sensitises staff as well as causing patient anaphylaxis.
  • Anaesthetists have a higher rate of substance misuse than most specialties — access, stress and the knowledge to self-treat, with opioids and propofol prominent. Controlled drug accounting and a non-punitive route to help are the system responses.

The definitions that get tested

  • Opportunity cost is the benefit forgone elsewhere because the resource was used here — not the money spent. Every funded treatment displaces another.
  • One QALY = one year in full health. Utility from 1 (full health) to 0 (death), with some states below zero. Derived from instruments such as EQ-5D.
  • DALYs are years of healthy life LOST (years of life lost + years lived with disability) — so higher is worse, the opposite direction to a QALY.
  • ICER = ΔCost / ΔEffect, judged against a threshold. NICE uses about £20,000 to £30,000 per QALY, with flexibility at end of life and for highly specialised technologies.
  • Marginal cost is the cost of one ADDITIONAL patient, usually much less than the average because fixed costs are already incurred.
  • Discounting future costs and benefits, conventionally 3.5% in the UK, systematically disadvantages prevention.
  • An option both cheaper and more effective DOMINATES and needs no threshold judgement. Equity and efficiency frequently conflict — economic evaluation informs the trade-off, it does not resolve it.

Applied to anaesthesia

  • Drug acquisition cost is a small fraction of perioperative cost. A valid comparison of techniques must include equipment, disposables, recovery time, PONV, unplanned admission and length of stay. Theatre time is the dominant variable cost.
  • Cancelled operations and unplanned critical care admissions are the largest avoidable cost drivers — the economic argument for preoperative assessment clinics and risk stratification.
  • Day-case surgery and enhanced recovery reduce cost and length of stay, provided they do not generate readmission, which erases the saving quickly.
  • Rationing is unavoidable; the question is whether it is explicit and accountable or implicit through waiting lists. Beveridge systems are tax-funded, Bismarck systems social-insurance funded.

VTE prophylaxis

Virchow's triad — stasis, endothelial injury, hypercoagulability — and surgery supplies all three. Risk assessment is required for all hospital admissions, medical as well as surgical, repeated within 24 h and on any change of state.

MeasureDetail
MechanicalGraduated compression stockings, intermittent pneumatic compression (the best evidence), foot impulse devices. Preferred when bleeding risk is high
After strokeStockings do NOT work and cause skin harm (CLOTS) — intermittent pneumatic compression is what reduces thrombosis and is recommended
PharmacologicalLMWH first-line (enoxaparin 40 mg or dalteparin 5,000 u daily), adjusted at weight extremes. Renally cleared, so reduce or use UFH in severe renal impairment
MonitoringLMWH does not reliably prolong the APTT — use anti-Xa if monitoring is needed. APTT is for unfractionated heparin
Duration28 days after major abdominal or pelvic cancer surgery; 28 to 35 days after hip arthroplasty, around 14 after knee
ContraindicationsActive bleeding, acute haemorrhagic stroke, platelets < 50 × 10⁹/L, untreated inherited bleeding disorder — these push towards mechanical methods alone

Neuraxial timing — learn the four numbers

  • Prophylactic LMWH: 12 hours before, 4 hours after. Treatment dose: 24 hours before.
  • Catheter removal carries the same risk as insertion and obeys the same intervals — the most commonly forgotten point.
  • Protamine is not used to enable a neuraxial block. It only partially reverses LMWH. Timing, not reversal, is the safeguard.
  • Vertebral canal haematoma: escalating back pain, unexpectedly dense or progressing motor block, block that fails to regress. Urgent MRI and decompression within 8 hours — outcome depends on the speed of surgery.

The rest

  • Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia: platelet fall at 5 to 10 days, with thrombosis rather than bleeding, from an antibody to the heparin–platelet factor 4 complex. Stop all heparin including flushes and give argatroban, danaparoid or fondaparinux. Do not start warfarin alone acutely — the early fall in protein C can cause venous limb gangrene.
  • DOACs are licensed after elective hip and knee arthroplasty, and are contraindicated in pregnancy and breastfeeding, where LMWH is the agent of choice and warfarin is teratogenic.
  • The puerperium carries a higher daily risk than pregnancy, highest after caesarean section, with prophylaxis for up to 6 weeks in high-risk women.
  • IVC filters reduce pulmonary embolism but increase later DVT, with no mortality benefit — a last resort, preferably retrievable, not an alternative to anticoagulation.
  • Regional anaesthesia, early mobilisation and avoiding dehydration all reduce VTE, and written patient information before discharge matters because much hospital-acquired thrombosis presents after the patient has gone home.
