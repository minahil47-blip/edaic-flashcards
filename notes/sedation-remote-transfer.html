Sedation, MRI, transfer, major incidents and the vertebral column

Anaesthesia away from the operating theatre, moving a critically ill patient safely, the CBRN material, and the spinal anatomy that underpins every neuraxial block.

Procedural sedation

Sedation is a continuum with no sharp boundaries, so the practitioner must be able to rescue a patient from one level deeper than intended.

LevelResponseAirway / ventilation
MinimalNormal to verbal commandUnaffected
ModeratePurposeful to verbal or light touchPatent, adequate spontaneous ventilation. Reflex withdrawal from pain does not count
DeepOnly to repeated or painful stimulationMay need intervention; ventilation may be inadequate. Same facilities and skills as general anaesthesia

Monitoring — the one point that matters most

  • Capnography detects apnoea and obstruction before the saturation falls. Pulse oximetry is a late monitor of ventilation: desaturation lags apnoea by minutes.
  • Supplemental oxygen delays desaturation but conceals hypoventilation — the saturation can stay at 100% through profound hypercapnia. This is the strongest single argument for capnography in sedation.
  • Most serious sedation morbidity is respiratory, compounded by inadequate monitoring and failure to rescue. It is a systems problem more than a drug problem.

The drugs

  • Midazolam has no analgesic action — anxiolysis, sedation, anterograde amnesia. Titrate in small increments with a 2 to 3 minute wait; the commonest cause of oversedation is the next increment given too soon. Much smaller doses in the elderly.
  • Benzodiazepine + opioid is synergistic for respiratory depression, far worse than either alone, and the commonest drug pattern in sedation incidents.
  • Flumazenil has a shorter half-life than midazolam — resedation, so observe at least 2 hours. Seizure risk in chronic benzodiazepine use and in mixed tricyclic overdose, so not a routine reversal agent.
  • Propofol: narrow margin, no antagonist — given by a dedicated person trained to rescue the airway. TCI does not remove the risk of unintended general anaesthesia: the model predicts, it does not measure.
  • Ketamine: dissociative, relatively preserved airway tone and respiration (preserved is not guaranteed), analgesia, nystagmus, hypersalivation, raised heart rate and pressure, emergence phenomena.
  • Dexmedetomidine: rousable sedation, little respiratory depression; bradycardia and hypotension limit it, onset is slow. Useful for awake fibreoptic intubation and awake craniotomy.
  • 50% nitrous oxide is contraindicated in pneumothorax, bowel obstruction, recent intraocular gas and decompression illness. Remimazolam gives ester-metabolised offset with flumazenil reversibility.

Governance

  • The same standards as general anaesthesia: assessment, fasting, consent, monitoring, trained assistance, suction, oxygen, a tipping trolley, resuscitation equipment and a staffed recovery area.
  • Deep sedation requires a dedicated practitioner whose sole responsibility is the patient. The operator-sedationist model is restricted to minimal and conscious sedation in defined settings.
  • Sedation is often MORE dangerous than general anaesthesia in a known difficult airway — an obstructed, apnoeic, sedated patient in an awkward position with no secured airway is the worst place to be.
  • Anaesthetist-delivered sedation for OSA, ASA III–IV, poor reserve, predicted difficult airway, long or painful procedures and extremes of age. Children have higher failure and adverse-event rates.
  • Discharge is criteria-led with a responsible adult, written instructions, and no driving or important decisions for 24 hours. Good local anaesthesia may remove the need for sedation altogether — sedation complements it, never substitutes for it.

Remote locations & MRI safety

Four zones; zones 1 and 2 are freely accessible, zone 3 is controlled, zone 4 is the scanner room. Everyone entering zones 3 and 4 is screened — staff and porters included.

FieldHazard
StaticProjectile / missile effect. The magnet is ALWAYS on, scanning or not, powered or not. The 0.5 mT (5 gauss) line marks the perimeter
GradientAcoustic noise > 100 dB from coil switching — ear protection is mandatory even for the anaesthetised patient, and the noise masks alarms. Also peripheral nerve stimulation
RadiofrequencyHeating and burns at conductive loops and wires. Cables kept straight, loop-free and off the skin; metallic-backed patches and some tattoos heat
QuenchReleases HELIUM, which displaces oxygen and can pressurise the room so the door will not open. Needs a quench pipe, an oxygen monitor and an evacuation plan

MR labelling and the resuscitation rule

  • MR safe — no hazard in any MR environment. MR unsafe — never in zone 4. MR conditional — safe only under stated conditions of field strength, gradient, specific absorption rate and distance, which must be read and checked each time. "Conditional" does not mean "safe".
  • A patient in cardiac arrest must be REMOVED from zone 4 immediately to a designated safe area. The resuscitation trolley and defibrillator never enter the magnet room, and the evacuation route is planned in advance for exactly this.
  • Pacemakers: many modern devices are MR conditional with cardiology programming, but abandoned or epicardial leads, older devices, cochlear implants, certain aneurysm clips and intraocular metallic foreign bodies remain contraindications.
  • Anaesthetic equipment in zone 4 must be MR conditional — aluminium cylinders, long circuits, extended cables, or equipment sited outside with a repeater display. Gadolinium can cause nephrogenic systemic fibrosis in severe renal impairment.

Anaesthesia away from theatre, generally

  • The standard of monitoring, assistance, equipment check and recovery is IDENTICAL regardless of location. Remote sites are riskier, not less demanding — and NAP4 found disproportionately severe airway complications outside theatre.
  • Recurring difficulties: patient inaccessible, dim lighting, noise, distance from help, unfamiliar staff and equipment, no second anaesthetist. A pre-procedure team brief with an explicit plan for deterioration is as valuable here as in theatre, arguably more so.
  • Cath lab and interventional radiology: radiation, contrast load, prolonged immobility, arms above the head, a moving table, and the anaesthetist distant from the airway. Iodinated contrast causes anaphylaxis and contrast nephropathy; hydration is the main prevention.
  • Radiotherapy in children may mean daily anaesthesia with the child entirely alone in the room, monitored by CCTV and remote displays.
  • Moving the patient into and out of the scanner is the high-risk moment for line and tube displacement — a checklist and a nominated person watching the airway.

Transfer of the critically ill

Primary (scene to hospital), secondary (hospital to hospital), intra-hospital — the last being commonest and most often under-prepared.

  1. 1

    Package before you go

    Most deterioration in transit is predictable. Stabilise in the referring hospital where there are resources — secure the airway if there is any doubt, rather than attempting it in a moving ambulance or a lift. Except for the rare time-critical transfer for haemorrhage control or neurosurgical decompression, stabilising reduces overall risk.

  2. 2

    Oxygen and equipment

    Calculate: minute volume × FiO₂ × duration, then at least double it, remembering the ventilator's own driving gas. Charged batteries plus spares, mains where available, and everything physically secured — an unsecured monitor is a projectile.

  3. 3

    Monitor properly

    ECG, oximetry, blood pressure, capnography (mandatory if intubated) and temperature. An arterial line beats NIBP, which is degraded by vibration and movement artefact and is only intermittent.

  4. 4

    Team, checklist, handover

    At least two people, one trained and experienced in transfer, insured, with a phone and a means of return. A pre-departure checklist reduces adverse events. The transfer is not complete until the structured handover is, with the transfer record of observations and interventions en route.

Physics and physiology in transit

  • Acceleration and deceleration cause real cardiovascular shifts; the patient normally travels head towards the front. Smooth driving matters more than speed — blue-light high-speed driving increases crash risk and physiological disturbance without improving outcome.
  • Air transfer: gas expands as ambient pressure falls (Boyle's law). Drain a pneumothorax first. Fill tracheal cuffs with saline, or monitor pressure continuously. Leave the nasogastric tube OPEN to vent expanding gastric gas, and never clamp a chest drain — it needs a one-way valve.
  • Cabin altitude is equivalent to around 2,400 m, so patients marginal at sea level need a higher FiO₂ or a cabin altitude restriction.
  • Head injury: intubate if GCS ≤ 8, normoxia, PaCO₂ 4.5 to 5.0 kPa, maintained MAP, 30° head up, no hypotonic fluid. Hyperventilation to 3.5 kPa is a short temporising measure for impending herniation only — sustained hypocapnia causes cerebral ischaemia.
  • A rigid collar impedes cerebral venous drainage and raises ICP, so manual in-line stabilisation or blocks and tape may be preferable in the head-injured patient.
  • Hypothermia is common and worsens coagulopathy in trauma — active warming, insulation and temperature monitoring are part of the package.
  • Commonest adverse events: equipment failure, tube or line displacement, hypotension and hypoxaemia — the great majority judged preventable.

Trauma physiology in a major incident

  • <C>ABC — catastrophic haemorrhage before airway in blast and ballistic injury: tourniquet, haemostatic dressing, pelvic binder.
  • Damage control resuscitation: permissive hypotension until control (not in head injury), balanced blood products rather than crystalloid, tranexamic acid within 3 h, warming, calcium. Then damage control surgery — arrest bleeding and contamination, correct physiology, return later.
  • Blast injury: primary from the wave itself, affecting gas-containing organs (ear, lung, bowel); secondary fragments; tertiary thrown; quaternary burns, crush, inhalation; quinary additive effects. Blast lung may declare itself hours later — ventilate with low pressures, as positive pressure can provoke air embolism.
  • Crush syndrome: hyperkalaemia, myoglobinuria, AKI on reperfusion. Fluid before extrication where possible. Suxamethonium must be avoided — it can precipitate arrest.

CBRN — the four things that are reliably asked

  • Decontaminate before entry. Disrobing removes the large majority of chemical contamination. A contaminated patient brought into the resuscitation room closes the department and makes casualties of the staff.
  • Nerve agents (sarin, VX, novichok) inhibit acetylcholinesterase: miosis, bronchorrhoea, bronchospasm, salivation, vomiting, incontinence, fasciculation, seizures. Atropine is titrated against DRYING OF SECRETIONS and ventilation, not heart rate, and may need enormous cumulative doses. Add pralidoxime (before enzyme ageing) and a benzodiazepine for seizures. Avoid suxamethonium — greatly prolonged.
  • Cyanide: histotoxic hypoxia from cytochrome oxidase inhibition — high lactate with a HIGH venous oxygen saturation. Hydroxocobalamin, or dicobalt edetate and sodium thiosulfate. Sulphur mustard: delayed blistering, no antidote, decontamination and support.
  • Irradiation vs contamination. A patient who has been irradiated but not contaminated is NOT a source and poses no hazard to staff. Only contamination needs protection and decontamination. Potassium iodide blocks thyroid uptake of radioiodine only — it does nothing for external irradiation or other radionuclides.
  • Working in full PPE degrades dexterity, vision and communication and causes heat stress — rehearse in it, and time-limit with rotation. Psychological casualties and staff psychological injury often outnumber physical casualties, and need planned support rather than single-session debriefing.

The midline approach, in order

  • Skin → subcutaneous fat → supraspinous ligament → INTERSPINOUS ligament → ligamentum flavum → epidural space → dura → arachnoid. Interspinous comes before ligamentum flavum, which is the last and densest structure.
  • The posterior longitudinal ligament is NEVER crossed — it lies on the posterior surface of the vertebral bodies, anterior to the canal.
  • Ligamentum flavum is thickest in the lumbar midline and may be incomplete in the cervical midline — one reason loss of resistance is less distinct there and the paramedian approach is preferred in the cervical and thoracic spine, where the spinous processes are also steeply angled caudally.
  • Skin-to-epidural depth in the adult lumbar region is typically 4 to 6 cm; record it whenever a catheter is sited.
  • Landmarks: C7 vertebra prominens · inferior angle of scapula ≈ T7 · intercristal ≈ L4 · posterior superior iliac spines ≈ S2. Dermatomes: nipple T4, xiphisternum T6, umbilicus T10, groin L1 — T4 for caesarean section, T10 for TURP.

Blood supply — the asymmetry to memorise

  • ONE anterior spinal artery and TWO posterior spinal arteries. The single anterior vessel supplies the anterior two thirds of the cord, including the anterior horns and the corticospinal and spinothalamic tracts.
  • Artery of Adamkiewicz — the dominant feeder to the lower cord, usually arising on the LEFT between T9 and L2, with variable origin. The reason thoracoabdominal aortic surgery risks paraplegia.
  • Anterior spinal artery syndrome: motor paralysis plus loss of pain and temperature, with preserved proprioception and vibration (dorsal columns are supplied posteriorly). That dissociated pattern is what identifies it.
  • The mid-thoracic watershed has a precarious supply; cord ischaemia follows prolonged hypotension, aortic cross-clamping or sacrifice of segmental vessels. CSF drainage and maintained perfusion pressure are protective.
  • Eight cervical nerves but seven cervical vertebrae — C1 to C7 emerge above their vertebra, C8 below C7, and from T1 down each nerve emerges below its numbered vertebra.
  • Block order: sympathetic highest, then sensory, then motor — the classic two-dermatome difference, and why hypotension precedes anything else.
