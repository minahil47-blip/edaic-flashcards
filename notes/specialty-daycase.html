Vascular, ENT, ophthalmic, day-case and blood management

The four specialty areas that generate disproportionate exam questions, plus patient blood management — the alternative to transfusing on the day.

Cardiac risk, as the exam asks it

  • Vascular surgery is high-risk surgery by definition (> 5% cardiac event rate for aortic and major peripheral vascular work).
  • Continue beta blockers and statins; do not start high-dose beta blockade de novo shortly before surgery — POISE showed fewer infarcts but more strokes and more deaths.
  • Functional capacity of < 4 METS identifies the patient who needs further assessment. CPET gives an anaerobic threshold and VO₂ peak.
  • Antiplatelet therapy after coronary stents: continue aspirin wherever possible, and delay elective non-cardiac surgery — conventionally at least 1 month after a bare metal stent and 6 months (minimum 3 in some guidance) after a drug-eluting stent.

Intraocular gas — the question that recurs

  • After intravitreal sulphur hexafluoride (SF₆) or perfluoropropane (C₃F₈) injection, nitrous oxide is absolutely contraindicated — it expands the bubble and can cause irreversible blindness.
  • The exclusion period is long: of the order of 2 to 4 weeks for SF₆ and up to 3 months for C₃F₈. The patient should carry a warning card.

Day-case anaesthesia

The modern principle: day surgery is the default for suitable procedures, and the question is not "is this patient fit for day case?" but "what must be optimised so they can be?"

  1. 1

    Patient selection

    Driven by functional status and stability, not by ASA grade or by age. A stable ASA III patient is suitable; an unstable ASA II is not. BMI alone is not an exclusion. Needs a responsible adult, reasonable travel time and a telephone.

  2. 2

    The real obstacles

    Pain, nausea and vomiting, and drowsiness cause most unplanned admissions — all anaesthetic, and all preventable. Surgical bleeding and urinary retention account for much of the rest.

  3. 3

    The anaesthetic

    Short-acting agents, multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia with paracetamol, NSAID and local anaesthesia or a regional block, PONV prophylaxis by risk score, and avoidance of long-acting opioids and of nitrous oxide where risk is high.

  4. 4

    Discharge

    Criteria-led, not time-led. Stable observations, orientated, able to walk, pain and nausea controlled, oral fluids tolerated, minimal bleeding, with written instructions and a contact number. Drinking and voiding are no longer mandatory for all patients.

PONV — Apfel and the drugs

  • Apfel's four risk factors: female sex, non-smoker, previous PONV or motion sickness, and postoperative opioids. Each adds roughly 20%: 10 · 20 · 40 · 60 · 80%.
  • Mechanisms differ, so combinations work: ondansetron (5-HT₃), dexamethasone, droperidol or haloperidol (D₂), cyclizine (H₁), aprepitant (NK₁), with TIVA using propofol itself antiemetic.
  • Risk reduction matters as much as prophylaxis: regional rather than general anaesthesia, avoid nitrous oxide and volatiles in high-risk patients, minimise opioids, adequate hydration.
  • Day-case spinal anaesthesia is feasible with short-acting agents (2-chloroprocaine, prilocaine) — hyperbaric bupivacaine delays discharge, and transient neurological symptoms are associated with lidocaine.

Anaemia & patient blood management

Three pillars: detect and treat anaemia, minimise blood loss, optimise the patient's tolerance of anaemia. Transfusion is the last resort, not the first intervention.

StepDetail
Define anaemiaWHO: Hb < 130 g/L in men, < 120 g/L in non-pregnant women. Many guidelines now use < 130 g/L for both sexes preoperatively, since the threshold for needing transfusion does not differ by sex
Screen earlyAt least 4 to 6 weeks before major surgery, so there is time to treat. Anaemia is an independent risk factor for morbidity, mortality and transfusion
InvestigateFerritin, transferrin saturation, CRP, B12, folate, renal function. Ferritin is an acute-phase protein, so a normal ferritin with TSAT < 20% still means functional iron deficiency
TreatOral iron if there is time (and better tolerated on alternate days); intravenous iron when surgery is within weeks, when oral iron is not tolerated, or in inflammation. Erythropoietin in selected renal and oncology patients
Minimise lossMeticulous surgery, tranexamic acid, cell salvage, point-of-care viscoelastic testing, normothermia, avoiding dilutional coagulopathy, and reducing diagnostic phlebotomy
Transfuse by triggerRestrictive threshold Hb 70 g/L (80 g/L in acute coronary syndrome or cardiac surgery), single unit then reassess. Restrictive is non-inferior or better in almost every trial

Transfusion risks worth being precise about

  • TACO (circulatory overload) is now the commonest cause of transfusion-related death — more than TRALI. It is largely preventable by assessment, slower rates and diuretics.
  • TRALI — non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema within 6 h, with normal filling pressures.
  • Acute haemolytic reaction is nearly always an identification error — the bedside check is the last defence (see Blood groups).
  • Febrile non-haemolytic reactions are common and benign; bacterial contamination of platelets is the infectious risk that matters most, as platelets are stored at room temperature.
  • Jehovah's Witness patients: cell salvage in a continuous circuit, erythropoietin, iron and tranexamic acid are usually acceptable; the decision is individual and must be documented in advance. A competent adult's refusal is binding even if death follows.
