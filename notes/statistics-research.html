Statistics & research methods

Study design, diagnostic test performance, p-values and power, risk measures, survival analysis and evidence appraisal — the whole of Paper A statistics in one place.

Study design

The hierarchy runs from case report up to systematic review of RCTs. What the examiner wants is the direction of enquiry: does the study start from the exposure or from the outcome?

DesignDirectionGives you
Cross-sectionalSnapshot, no time axisPrevalence. Cannot establish causation
Case–controlOutcome → backwards to exposureOdds ratio only. Good for rare diseases; prone to recall and selection bias
CohortExposure → forwards to outcomeIncidence, relative risk. Good for rare exposures; loss to follow-up is the weakness
RCTAllocated exposure → outcomeCausation. Randomisation balances unknown confounders — the thing no observational design can do
CrossoverEach patient is their own controlHigh power with few patients. Needs a washout; useless for conditions that progress or cure

The high-yield distinctions

  • Case–control gives an odds ratio, never a relative risk — you cannot calculate incidence when you chose how many cases to recruit.
  • Randomisation deals with confounding; blinding deals with measurement and performance bias. Different jobs, often confused.
  • Allocation concealment (the recruiter cannot know the next assignment) is not the same as blinding, and matters more.
  • Intention-to-treat analyses patients as randomised and preserves the benefit of randomisation. Per-protocol exaggerates efficacy. ITT is the primary analysis for a superiority trial; non-inferiority trials need both.
  • Equipoise — genuine uncertainty about which arm is better — is the ethical precondition for randomising at all.

Bias, in the forms that get examined

  • Selection — who got in. Performance — different care between arms. Detection — outcome assessed differently. Attrition — differential dropout. Reporting — selective outcomes.
  • Confounding is a third variable associated with both exposure and outcome. Handled by randomisation, matching, stratification or multivariable adjustment — never by a bigger sample.
  • A larger sample reduces random error but not bias. This single sentence answers a great many statements.
  • Hawthorne effect — behaviour changes because of observation. Regression to the mean — extreme values drift back without any treatment.

SnNOut and SpPIn

  • Snsitive test, Negative result → rules the disease Out. A sensitive test is the screening test.
  • Specific test, Positive result → rules the disease In. A specific test is the confirmatory test.
  • Raising the cut-off raises specificity and lowers sensitivity, and the reverse. They always trade against one another — the ROC curve is the picture of that trade.
  • Area under the ROC curve: 0.5 is useless (the diagonal), 1.0 is perfect. It is a prevalence-independent summary of discrimination.
  • Likelihood ratios are the prevalence-proof way to express a test, which is exactly why they are preferred to predictive values.

p-values, errors and power

  1. p

    What a p-value is

    The probability of observing data at least as extreme as this if the null hypothesis were true. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and not the probability the result arose by chance.

  2. I

    Type I error — α

    Rejecting a true null: a false positive. Conventionally set at 0.05. Testing many outcomes inflates it, which is what Bonferroni and other corrections address.

  3. II

    Type II error — β

    Failing to reject a false null: a false negative. Usually set at 0.1 to 0.2.

  4. 1−β

    Power

    Power = 1 − β, conventionally 80 to 90%. Increased by a larger sample, a larger effect size, less variance, a higher α, or paired/crossover design.

Traps

  • "Not significant" does not mean "no difference" — it may mean the study was underpowered. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
  • Statistical significance is not clinical significance. A huge trial can make a trivial difference significant.
  • The confidence interval is more informative than the p-value, because it shows the size and precision of the effect. A 95% CI excluding the null is equivalent to p < 0.05.
  • Power is calculated before the study, not after. A post-hoc power calculation on one's own negative result is circular.
  • Confidence interval width narrows with the square root of the sample size — quadrupling n halves the interval.

Choosing the test

  • Parametric (t-test, ANOVA, Pearson) needs normally distributed, continuous data with similar variances. Unpaired vs paired t-test for independent vs related samples; ANOVA for three or more groups.
  • Non-parametric for ordinal data or non-normal distributions: Mann–Whitney U (unpaired), Wilcoxon signed rank (paired), Kruskal–Wallis (≥ 3 groups), Spearman for correlation.
  • Categorical: chi-squared, with Fisher's exact when expected cell counts are small (< 5).
  • SD describes the spread of the data; SEM describes the precision of the mean — SEM = SD / √n, so SEM shrinks with sample size and SD does not. Quoting SEM to look tidy is a classic misuse.
  • Normal distribution: ±1 SD ≈ 68%, ±2 SD ≈ 95%, ±3 SD ≈ 99.7%. Mean = median = mode; skew is zero.

Risk measures

Work from two risks: control event rate (CER) and experimental event rate (EER).

Absolute risk reductionCER − EERThe clinically meaningful number
Number needed to treat1 / ARRAlways round up. Quote with the time frame and the outcome
Odds ratio(a/c) / (b/d) = ad / bcApproximates RR only when the outcome is rare; otherwise it exaggerates

Worked example to carry into the exam

  • Mortality 10% control, 5% treated. RR = 0.5, RRR = 50%, ARR = 5%, NNT = 20.
  • Now mortality 0.2% vs 0.1%. RRR is still 50% — but ARR = 0.1% and NNT = 1000. Same relative claim, utterly different clinical value.
  • An RR of 1, an OR of 1, or a 95% CI that crosses 1 all mean no demonstrated effect. For a difference in means the null value is 0, not 1.

Survival analysis

  • Kaplan–Meier curves plot the probability of surviving over time as a step function, stepping down at each event. They handle censoring — patients lost to follow-up or still alive at the end contribute information up to the point they left.
  • Median survival is read where the curve crosses 0.5. It is reportable even when many patients are still alive, which the mean is not — the single biggest advantage of the method.
  • Log-rank test compares two or more curves. It tests whether the curves differ; it gives no estimate of the size of the difference.
  • Cox proportional hazards regression gives a hazard ratio adjusted for covariates. Its central assumption is that the hazard ratio is constant over time — if the curves cross, that assumption fails.
  • A hazard ratio is an instantaneous relative risk of the event, not a ratio of total deaths, and not a ratio of survival times.
  • Censoring must be independent of outcome. If patients drop out because they are dying, the curve is biased — this is informative censoring.
  • Numbers at risk should be printed beneath the curve: the right-hand tail of a Kaplan–Meier plot is usually built on very few patients and should not be over-interpreted.

Evidence & meta-analysis

ConceptWhat to know
Systematic reviewA pre-specified protocol, explicit search, duplicate screening, risk-of-bias assessment. A meta-analysis is the optional statistical pooling on the end — a review need not have one, and should not if the studies are too heterogeneous
Forest plotEach study a square (size = weight) with its CI; the diamond is the pooled estimate. The vertical line of no effect is at 1 for ratios and 0 for differences
HeterogeneityI² is the proportion of variation due to real between-study difference, not chance: roughly < 25% low, > 75% high. Cochran's Q tests it. High I² pushes you to a random-effects model and to subgroup or sensitivity analysis
Fixed vs random effectsFixed assumes one true effect; random effects assumes a distribution of true effects, gives wider intervals and weights small studies relatively more
Funnel plotEffect against precision. Asymmetry suggests publication bias — small negative studies missing. It is a weak test with few studies, and asymmetry has other causes
GRADERates certainty as high / moderate / low / very low. RCTs start high and are downgraded for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias; observational studies start low and can be upgraded

Reporting guidelines — know which is which

  • CONSORT — randomised trials. PRISMA — systematic reviews. STROBE — observational studies. STARD — diagnostic accuracy. CARE — case reports.
  • Trial registration before recruitment (ClinicalTrials.gov, ISRCTN) is what makes selective outcome reporting detectable, and is now a condition of publication.
  • Research governance: Declaration of Helsinki, ethics committee approval, informed consent, data protection, and declaration of conflicts of interest.

Two things that are not the same

  • Internal validity — is the result true for the patients studied? External validity / generalisability — does it apply to yours? A flawless trial in fit 40-year-olds may be irrelevant to your 85-year-old.
  • Efficacy — does it work under ideal trial conditions? Effectiveness — does it work in ordinary practice? Pragmatic trials target the second.
